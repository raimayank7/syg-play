Overs and balls: how cricket counts

Cricket does not write overs as ordinary decimals. The number after the dot counts balls, and since an over has six balls it can only be 0 to 5. So 12.4 is twelve overs and four balls, and 12.6 never appears: it would be 13.0.

Turn overs into balls

Multiply the whole overs by 6 and add the balls: 12.4 = 12 × 6 + 4 = 76 balls. Do this first whenever you need a rate.

Turn balls into a decimal over

Divide by 6: 76 ÷ 6 = 12.667. This is the figure for a rate sum. 120 runs in 12.4 overs is 120 ÷ 12.667 = 9.47 an over; typing 12.4 would give 9.68 and be wrong.

Adding overs

Add balls, then convert back. 8.5 + 3.4 = 53 balls + 22 balls = 75 balls = 12.3 overs (12 overs and 3 balls). Adding as ordinary decimals would give 11.9, which is not even a valid number of overs.

Overs left

In a 50-over innings with 36.2 overs gone, 36.2 is 218 balls, so 300 − 218 = 82 balls remain, which is 13.667 overs or 13.4 in cricket notation.

Subtracting overs

Subtraction works the same way. Overs left after 36.2 in a 50-over innings: 300 − 218 = 82 balls, which is 13.4 in cricket notation. A bowler who has bowled 2.3 overs of a 4-over spell has 9 balls left. Convert to balls, subtract, and convert back; never subtract the decimals directly.

Why the convention exists

The dot notation is easy to read from a scoreboard: you see at once how far into the over the bowler is. It is awkward for spreadsheets, which treat the dot as decimal. That is why the calculators on this site take overs the cricket way and convert for you.
