How net run rate decides who qualifies

Suppose three teams in a 20-over league are all on the same number of points before the last round. The table is decided by net run rate, so every run counts. Here is how to read the situation, with numbers worked out from three matches each.

The three teams

TeamRate forRate againstNet run rate
Hawks7.676.87+0.800
Lions7.887.47+0.415
Tigers7.927.75+0.167

Hawks lead the others by 0.385, and Lions are second. To overtake them in the next match, the team behind needs to improve its figures by more than that gap, not by a single run: it must score faster, concede slower, or both.

How much does one match move the number?

With about 60 overs already in each team's figures, a new 20-over match adds a quarter of that again. A win by 30 runs improves the net run rate by roughly 30 ÷ 20 ÷ 4 ≈ 0.4 for a side that bowls its 20 overs, because the difference of 1.5 runs an over only counts in one-quarter of the total overs. This is why one heavy win or one narrow defeat can swing a table so much after only a few matches.

Chasing: finish early

A team that wins a chase with overs to spare faced fewer overs, so its rate for goes up. If the target is 150 and a team gets there in 15 overs it has scored at 10 an over; at 20 overs it would have been 7.5. Early finishes help both the batting figure and the margin.

Practical advice for a local league

  • Keep one running sheet with the runs and overs of every match, not only the results.
  • Update the table with the points table maker after each round so every captain sees where they stand.
  • Agree the tie-break rule before the tournament starts, including what happens in rain-shortened matches.
