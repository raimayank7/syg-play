Cricket formulas cheat sheet

All the common cricket sums on one page, with a link to the calculator for each. Remember the one rule that catches everyone: overs are written 12.4 for 12 overs and 4 balls, so turn them into balls or a decimal before dividing.

How the numbers connect

Bowling average = economy rate × bowling strike rate ÷ 6, because runs per wicket is runs per over times overs per wicket. For a batter, runs per over = strike rate × 6 ÷ 100. A team's run rate is simply the sum of what its batters score per over.

Lower or higher is better?

Higher is better for strike rate, batting average and net run rate. Lower is better for economy rate and bowling average and bowling strike rate. The required rate is neither: it just tells you what the situation demands.

Rounding

Scorers usually give averages and rates to two decimal places and net run rate to three. We follow that. Rounding earlier in a long sum can change the third decimal of a net run rate, which is why the calculator keeps full precision until the end.

One innings, every formula

A batter scores 60 off 40 balls and is out. Strike rate = 60 ÷ 40 × 100 = 150.00. A bowler concedes 32 runs in 4 overs and takes 2 wickets: economy = 32 ÷ 4 = 8.00, average = 32 ÷ 2 = 16.00, strike rate = 24 balls ÷ 2 = 12.0. A team scores 168 in 20 overs: run rate 8.40. The opposing team needs 169 to win: after 12 overs at 90, required rate = 79 ÷ 8 = 9.88. Every one of these sums is a division of something by something, which is why the calculators ask only for two numbers each.

Where these are used

Strike rate and economy are the numbers most often quoted in short-format cricket, averages in long-format. Net run rate is the tie-breaker of league tables. None of the formulas is secret or owned by anyone: they are plain arithmetic, long used by scorers everywhere.
