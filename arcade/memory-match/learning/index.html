What Memory Match teaches

Memory games train short-term memory and attention. Young children learn to match pictures and take turns when they play with a parent. Older players find that systematic scanning and naming positions helps them in school, too. The animals on the cards are easy to name, so the game is friendly to children who are learning words.

The main skill: memory and attention

Every game asks something of the player. In Memory Match it is mostly memory and attention, with a little speed and attention on the side. The skill grows with practice, so a child who plays a few minutes a day for a month tends to play noticeably better, and the thinking habits carry over to schoolwork.

Ideas for parents and teachers

  • Let a young child play the small board with you and take turns, naming each animal.
  • Play without looking at the move counter, then compare scores at the end.
  • For older children, challenge them to finish the large board in under 45 moves.

Questions to ask while playing

Talking is where the learning happens. Try these with a child, a student or yourself:

  • Which cards have I seen only once?
  • Where was the twin of this card?
  • Which part of the board have I not turned over yet?
  • Is it better to explore or to match this time?

Which age is it for?

Memory Match is one of the first games children can play alone, from about four or five on the small board, because it needs only looking and remembering. The animals on the cards help children learn names and sounds. Older children can try the large board, and adults find it a gentle way to exercise short-term memory. Take turns with a young child, naming each animal as it appears.

A sensible amount

These games are made for short breaks of five to fifteen minutes. A good routine is to play for a few minutes after homework, then do something away from the screen. We show no pop-ups in the game, ask for no purchases and have no chat, so children are not distracted or contacted by others. See our note for parents.
