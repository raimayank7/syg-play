Tambola for kids: a number game for home and class

Tambola is one of the easiest games to turn into a lesson, because it is already full of numbers. Children who play it practise reading, hearing and recognising numbers without feeling that they are doing work.

By age

  • Ages 5 to 6: use numbers up to 20 on a small card, and let the child cross the number with a coloured pencil. The caller shows the number card as well as saying it, so the child sees and hears.
  • Ages 7 to 9: play with the full 1 to 90 ticket, and let a child take a turn as the caller. Calling out numbers clearly is good reading practice.
  • Ages 10 to 12: turn the call into a sum: instead of saying 48, say "six times eight" or "one more than forty-seven", and the players work out the number.

Games to try

  1. Odd or even: players cross numbers only if they are even.
  2. Tens column: a prize goes to the first child to complete the column of the nineties (80 to 90) or any column you name.
  3. Add and cross: the caller names two numbers, and players cross the sum if it is on their ticket.

Class tips

  • Print one strip for each group of six, so that every number is on somebody’s ticket.
  • Let the winner become the next caller.
  • Keep prizes small and let every child choose a sticker at the end.

The ticket maker prints tickets, and the caller works on a classroom screen too, with a board showing every number called. See the rules for the basic game.

A first lesson in reading numbers

Young children often mix up numbers that sound alike, such as thirteen and thirty. Tambola is excellent for this because the caller says each number twice, once whole and once digit by digit. After a few rounds many children begin to hear the difference between "teen" and "ty".

Making it safe and calm

  • Seat children close to the caller so they can hear.
  • Use big pens or counters that they can handle easily.
  • Praise the effort rather than the winning. Children who do not win can be given jobs, such as showing the called numbers on a board.

Taking it home

Parents can print the tickets from the ticket maker and play a short game of 15 minutes after dinner. Stopping while the children still want more means they ask for it again tomorrow.
