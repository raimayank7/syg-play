The birthday problem: how many people share a birthday?

Ask how many people you need in a room for two of them to share a birthday, and most guess about 180. The answer is 23: with 23 people the chance of at least one shared birthday is 50.7%. This page works it out and shows a table.

How it is worked out

It is easier to find the chance that nobody shares. The first person can have any birthday. The second must avoid one date: 364 out of 365. The third must avoid two: 363 out of 365, and so on. Multiply these fractions together, subtract from 1, and you have the table above. We assume 365 equally likely birthdays and ignore 29 February, which is the usual simplification.

Why it feels wrong

The question is not about someone sharing your birthday, which needs about 253 people for even odds. It is about any two people, and a group of 23 has 253 pairs. Each pair has a 1 in 365 chance, and with 253 chances those add up quickly.

Use it in class

Ask a class to write down birthdays and look for a match, then compare with the table. With 30 children the chance is about 71%, so most classes of that size have a match. It is a good first lesson in how counting pairs changes intuition. Our random number tool can simulate birthdays: pick 23 numbers from 1 to 365 and look for repeats.
