How to solve a nonogram

A nonogram (also called a picross or griddler puzzle) is a grid with numbers beside every row and column. Each number tells you the length of a run of filled squares in that line, in order, with at least one empty square between runs. When you fill the grid so that every clue is satisfied, a picture appears. This guide teaches the rules and the techniques that solve almost every puzzle without guessing.

The rules

  • A clue such as "3 1" means a run of three filled squares, then at least one empty square, then a run of one.
  • Runs appear in the order given, and squares outside the runs are empty.
  • A clue of 0 means the whole line is empty.

Technique 1: overlap

Take a line of 10 squares with a clue of 7. The run can start in square 1, 2, 3 or 4. Wherever it starts, squares 4 to 7 are always inside it, so you can fill them straight away. The rule: if the clue is bigger than half the line, the middle part is certain. If a clue (or the clues plus the gaps between them) fills the line exactly, you can fill the whole line.

Technique 2: cross out the impossible

When a row is complete (you have filled all its runs), cross out the rest. When an edge square is filled and the first clue is 3, the run must be squares 1 to 3, so square 4 is empty. Crossing out squares helps the columns that pass through them.

Technique 3: use the crossing lines

Every filled or crossed square you find is new information for the line that crosses it. Work back and forth: rows, then columns, then rows again. The solver on this site does exactly this, one line at a time, and puzzles here are accepted only if that is enough.

A worked 5 by 5 example

Clues for rows: 1 1 / 5 / 5 / 3 / 1. Clues for columns: 2 / 4 / 4 / 4 / 2.

When you are stuck

Re-read each line with its current squares: a line with one filled square and a clue of 1 is finished, and a line whose first unknown square cannot hold the next run must be crossed out.
