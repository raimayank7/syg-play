Tree nonogram

A 10 by 10 picture of a pine tree on a short trunk. Fill the squares to reveal it.

About this puzzle

The picture is a pine tree on a short trunk. It fills 48 of the 100 squares (48%) and has 24 clue numbers in all, the largest being 10. A solver that uses only line logic, the way you do, finishes it in 3 rounds of checking every row and column, so every square can be worked out without a guess. The picture was drawn for this site.

Hints

Begin with the biggest numbers: a row or column with a clue of 6 or more leaves some squares that must be filled whatever the arrangement. Mark squares you know are empty with a cross, then go back and forth between rows and columns. This one needs a few rounds, so be patient: each new square you find unlocks the crossing lines. Read the solving guide if you are stuck.

How the solver gets there

After the first round of checking every row and column, the solver knows 60 of the 100 squares; after round 2 it knows 96; after round 3 it knows 100. In every round it uses only the clues and what it has already found. Rows read from top to bottom have the clues 2 / 4 / 6 / 4 / 6 / 8 / 6 / 8 / 2 / 2. Columns read from left to right have the clues 0 / 1 1 / 1 4 / 7 / 10 / 10 / 7 / 1 4 / 1 1 / 0.

Using it with children

A nonogram is a gentle way to practise counting, reading numbers in order and thinking about rows and columns, which is also how coordinates on a map or in a spreadsheet work. Ask a child to say aloud why a square must be filled, rather than just filling it. When the picture appears, ask what else could be drawn on a 10 by 10 grid, and let them design a small puzzle on squared paper for you to solve.
