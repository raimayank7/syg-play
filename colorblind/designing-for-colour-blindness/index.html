Designing for colour blindness

About one man in twelve and one woman in two hundred has some form of colour vision difference (the figures are for people of northern European descent, and vary elsewhere). If you make a chart, a map, a traffic-light style indicator or a sign, those readers are part of your audience. This guide gives rules that work and a table, computed by program, of colour pairs that look different to most people and which of them are risky.

Rules that work

  1. Never use colour alone. Add shapes, labels, patterns or position as a second cue.
  2. Differ in lightness. Two colours of equal lightness merge for many viewers, whatever their hue.
  3. Avoid red against green, and green against brown or orange, for anything that carries meaning.
  4. Test it. Use the simulator or check the greyscale version: if the information survives in greyscale, it will survive most colour vision differences.
  5. Use a tested palette such as this one.

Common pairs, tested

The numbers are the distance between the two colours in linear colour space as seen with normal vision and as simulated for each type: bigger is easier to tell apart. A pair is marked hard when the distance falls to a quarter of the normal value or below 0.08. 6 of the 8 pairs are marked hard for at least one type.

Examples from daily life

Red-yellow-green status lights, red and green lines on a chart, and colour-coded wires or maps are the usual culprits. Put the status in words ("OK", "Late", "Failed") or shape next to the colour. For maps, use different patterns for areas that matter and lightness steps rather than a rainbow scale.
