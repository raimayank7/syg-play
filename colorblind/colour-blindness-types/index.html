Types of colour blindness

"Colour blindness" is an everyday name for a family of colour vision differences. Most people who have one can see colour; they see some colours as similar that others see as different. This page names the main types in plain words. It describes the usual medical classification and is not a diagnosis tool: a proper test is done by an optician or eye doctor.

The three cone types

The retina holds three kinds of colour-sensing cells, called cones: for long wavelengths (red), medium (green) and short (blue). Colour vision differences come from one kind of cone working differently or not at all.

Red-green types

  • Protanomaly and protanopia (red cones weak or missing): reds look darker and less red, and are confused with greens, browns and dark greys.
  • Deuteranomaly and deuteranopia (green cones weak or missing): the most common type. Greens look more red; reds, greens, browns and oranges are confused.

The endings matter: "-anomaly" means the cone works weakly, "-anopia" that it does not work. The red-green types are usually inherited and carried on the X chromosome, which is why they are much more common in men.

Blue-yellow type

Tritanomaly and tritanopia (blue cones weak or missing) are rare and affect men and women equally. Blues and greens look alike, and yellows and pinks are confused.

Total colour blindness

Achromatopsia is very rare: no or very little colour is seen and bright light is uncomfortable. Our simulator's "greyscale" row shows what a world without colour looks like, which is also a handy design test.

Living with it

Many people with red-green differences manage well and may not know they have one until a test. Careers that need accurate colour judgement, such as some in electrical wiring or aviation, test for it. Designers can help by following the rules on our design page.
