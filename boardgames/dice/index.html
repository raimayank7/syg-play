Dice roller and cowrie thrower

For the day the dice are under the sofa. Roll up to six dice, or throw cowrie shells for traditional games.

How to use it

Choose how many dice you need and tap Roll the dice. Each dice shows a number from 1 to 6, and the total is given underneath. Ludo and snakes and ladders use one dice; backgammon uses two. Tap again for the next roll. Nothing is stored and nothing is sent anywhere; the numbers come from your browser's own random number source, the same one used for security purposes, so each face is as likely as any other and no roll depends on the last.

Cowrie shells

Long before cubic dice were common, Indian games such as Pachisi and Chowka Bara were played by throwing cowrie shells and counting how many landed with the opening (the "mouth") facing up. The second roller here throws four to seven cowries. Each shell lands mouth up or mouth down with equal chance in our version, and the result shows how many are mouth up.

What a throw is worth depends on the game and on the region, so we show only the count. As an example of how traditional scoring works: in one widespread way of playing Chowka Bara with four cowries, one, two or three mouths up count as 1, 2 and 3; all four up count as 4, and all four down count as 8, and those last two throws earn another turn. Pachisi, with six or seven cowries, has its own table, which varies from place to place. If you are playing a family game, use the values your elders use. Real shells are not perfectly even, so real throws are a little different from ours.

Is a phone dice fair?

Yes, and usually fairer than a worn or cheap dice, whose rounded corners and hollowed spots can favour some faces slightly. What a screen cannot give is the feel of the throw, and with children that matters. Keep a real dice if you have one; use this page when it has rolled under the sofa.

Why sixes feel rare

Each face comes up one time in six on average, but "on average" allows long droughts. The chance of going six rolls without a six is about one in three, and going twelve rolls without one happens about once in nine tries. So a ludo player who waits many turns for a six is not unlucky, just normal. With two dice the totals are not equally likely: 7 can be made in six ways out of thirty-six and is the commonest total, while 2 and 12 can each be made in only one way.

Other ways to manage without dice

  • Six slips of paper numbered 1 to 6, drawn from a cup and put back each time.
  • A pencil with six sides: number the faces and roll it.
  • A spinner: a circle of card divided into six, with a pencil through the middle.
  • Playing cards: take the ace to six of one suit, shuffle, turn one over, and reshuffle each time.
  • Coins: three coins give a number from 0 to 3 heads; useful for small-step games, though the middle numbers come up more often.

Games that use dice or cowries
