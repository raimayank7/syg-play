Noughts and crosses (tic-tac-toe): rules and how never to lose

Players
2
You need
Paper and a pen
A game takes
Under a minute
Aim
Get three of your marks in a row

Noughts and crosses, called tic-tac-toe in America and known in India by names such as "zero-kata", is the first strategy game most children meet. The rules take ten seconds. What makes it worth a page is the discovery, which every child makes sooner or later, that it cannot be won against someone who knows how to play. Understanding why is a first lesson in thinking ahead.

The rules

  1. Draw two lines down and two lines across to make a grid of nine spaces.
  2. One player is X, the other O. X goes first.
  3. Players take turns to put their mark in an empty space.
  4. The first player to get three of their marks in a line, across, down or diagonally, wins.
  5. If all nine spaces are filled and nobody has a line, the game is a draw.

Swap who goes first after every game, since the first player has the advantage.

Why a good game is always a draw

There are eight possible lines: three rows, three columns and two diagonals. To win you must threaten two lines at once, because a single threat is simply blocked. A double threat is called a fork. The whole game is about making a fork, and stopping your opponent from making one. If both players do this correctly, neither succeeds and the board fills up.

How to play first (X)

  • Start in a corner. It gives the opponent the most chances to go wrong. If O answers anywhere except the centre, X can force a win.
  • If O takes the centre, take the opposite corner. If O then takes another corner, O has made a mistake and you can fork; if O takes an edge, the game heads for a draw.
  • Starting in the centre is also sound. If O answers on an edge (not a corner), X can force a win.

How to play second (O) and never lose

  • If X starts in a corner, take the centre. Then, if X takes the opposite corner, play an edge, not a corner: this forces X to block and breaks the fork.
  • If X starts in the centre, take a corner.
  • If X starts on an edge, take the centre.
  • After that, follow the checklist below on every move.

A checklist for every move

  1. Can I complete three in a row? Do it and win.
  2. Can my opponent complete three in a row next move? Block it.
  3. Can I make a fork, two lines of two with one mark? Do it.
  4. Can my opponent make a fork next move? Stop it, either by taking that space or by making a two-in-a-row that forces them to block somewhere harmless.
  5. Otherwise take the centre, then a corner, then an edge.

A player who follows this list never loses.

When the game stops being fun

Once two players both know the checklist, every game is a draw, and it is time to make the game bigger. That moment is a success, not a failure: the child has solved a game.

Variations that restore the challenge

  • Four in a row on a five by five or six by six grid.
  • Five in a row on open squared paper, a deep game with its own page: five in a row.
  • Three men's morris: each player has only three counters. After all six are placed, players take turns to slide a counter to a neighbouring empty space. The first line of three wins. See nine men's morris.
  • Misère: the player who makes three in a row loses.
  • Wild: on each turn a player may write either an X or an O; whoever completes a line of three of the same mark wins.
  • The big game: nine small grids arranged in a large three by three grid. Where you play in a small grid tells your opponent which small grid they must play in next; win three small grids in a row to win. This version is hard for adults.

For teachers

Ask a class to find out whether the first player can always win, and let them test their ideas against each other. Then ask them to write the rules for a player who never loses. It is a child-sized piece of real mathematics: the same idea, on a far greater scale, is how computers were taught to play chess.
