How to play reversi

Players
2
You need
A board of 64 squares and 64 discs, dark on one side and light on the other
A game takes
15 to 30 minutes
Aim
Have more discs of your colour when the board is full

Reversi takes a minute to learn and a lifetime to play well. Discs are dark on one side and light on the other. Each move traps a line of enemy discs between two of yours, and the trapped discs are turned over to become yours. The lead swings wildly, and a player who is far behind with ten moves to go can still win. The game was first sold in England in the 1880s; any coins or counters with two different faces will serve as discs, and the board can be an ordinary chess board.

Setting up

Place four discs on the four central squares, two of each colour, with like colours diagonal to each other. Each player takes a colour. Dark moves first.

How to move

  1. On your turn, place one disc, your colour up, on an empty square.
  2. The move is legal only if it outflanks at least one enemy disc: the new disc and another disc of your colour must have one or more enemy discs between them in an unbroken straight line, across, up and down, or diagonally, with no gaps.
  3. Turn over every enemy disc that is outflanked. They are now yours.
  4. One disc may outflank in several directions at once. All the lines are flipped; you may not choose to flip only some.
  5. Only discs trapped as a direct result of the disc just placed are flipped. Discs that become surrounded as a side effect of the flipping stay as they are.

Passing

If you have no legal move, you pass and your opponent moves again. You may not pass if a legal move exists, even if every move available is bad.

The end

The game ends when neither player can move, usually because the board is full. Count the discs: the player with more of their colour showing wins. An equal count is a draw.

Common mistakes about the rules

  • A disc placed must flip at least one enemy disc. You cannot place on a square just because it is empty.
  • Flipped discs do not cause further flips in the same turn.
  • The line of trapped discs must be continuous; an empty square breaks it.
  • Discs are never removed or moved, only turned over.

Strategy: why having fewer discs can be better

The natural urge is to flip as many discs as possible on every move. It is usually wrong. In the first two-thirds of the game, the number of discs you have means little. What matters is who has good moves left.

  • Corners are gold. A disc in a corner can never be flipped, because nothing can get beyond it. From a corner you can build rows along the edges that are also safe for ever.
  • So avoid the squares next to the corners, especially the one diagonally next to it. A disc there usually hands the corner to your opponent.
  • Keep your choices open. A player with few legal moves is soon forced to make a bad one. Early on, prefer quiet moves that flip few discs and leave your opponent little to play.
  • Do not rush to the edges. Edge discs are hard to flip, but an edge taken too early can be turned into a trap. Take edges when they are safe.
  • Count at the end. In the last ten moves, look for the sequence that gives you the last move in each region; then at last the number of discs is what counts.

A note on names

A closely related game is sold under a brand name; it always begins from the fixed central pattern shown above. In the oldest form of reversi the first four discs are placed by the players in turn on the central squares. This page describes the game with the fixed start, which is how nearly everyone plays today.

Questions people ask

Who goes first in reversi?

The player with the dark discs.

What if I cannot move?

You pass, and your opponent plays again. If neither player can move, the game is over.

Can a corner disc be flipped?

No. Nothing can be placed beyond a corner to outflank it, so a corner disc stays yours for the rest of the game.
