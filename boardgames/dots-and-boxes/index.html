How to play dots and boxes

Players
2 (3 or 4 also works)
You need
Paper and a pen each
A game takes
5 to 15 minutes
Aim
Complete more boxes than the other player

Dots and boxes is the classroom game played on the back page of every notebook. Two players take turns joining dots with a line, and whoever draws the fourth side of a little square owns it. For most players the game is a matter of not being the first to give boxes away. But underneath lies one of the cleverest strategies of any pencil game, and learning a small part of it will make you unbeatable in your class or family.

The rules

  1. Draw a square grid of dots. Five by five dots (sixteen boxes) is a quick game; six by six dots (twenty-five boxes) is a good standard.
  2. Players take turns to draw one line joining two neighbouring dots, across or down. Diagonals are not allowed.
  3. A player who draws the fourth side of a box claims it by writing their initial inside, and then must draw another line. If that line completes another box, they go again, and so on.
  4. When no lines are left to draw, the player with more boxes wins.

Note the word "must": after completing a box you have to move again, even when every line available gives something to your opponent.

How a normal game goes

At first, both players draw lines that do not create the third side of any box, because a box with three sides is a gift to the other player. Eventually every safe line is used up. The board has by then divided into regions: chains (corridors of boxes that will fall one after another once the first is opened) and loops. Now someone has to open a chain, and the opponent takes all its boxes. So far, so familiar.

The trick that wins: do not take everything

When your opponent opens a long chain for you, take all the boxes except the last two. Instead of taking those, draw the line at the far end that leaves them as a pair for your opponent. They take the two boxes with one line, and then, since they must move again, they have to open the next chain for you.

You gave away two boxes and received a whole chain in return. Do this at the end of every chain except the last, and you take nearly all the big ones. Players call the move a double-cross, and the player who can use it is said to be "in control". Being in control is worth more than any number of early boxes.

  • The trick works on chains of three or more boxes. On a chain of two there is nothing left to take after the sacrifice, though a careful opponent can still use a two-box chain against you.
  • For a closed loop, the price of keeping control is four boxes instead of two, so loops favour the player who is not in control.

How to get control

The player in control is the one who does not have to open the first long chain. That is decided by a count, and there is a simple rule of thumb for it. Count the dots in the grid and the number of long chains (three boxes or more) that the board will end up with:

  • If the total number of dots is even (for example a six by six grid, 36 dots), the first player should try to make the number of long chains odd, and the second player should try to make it even.
  • If the total number of dots is odd (a five by five grid, 25 dots), it is the other way round: the first player wants an even number of long chains.

So the middle of the game is a quiet battle over how many chains there will be. You make a chain by walling off a corridor; you destroy one by cutting it into pieces of one or two boxes, or by joining two together. You do not need to count perfectly to benefit: simply knowing that the number of chains matters puts you ahead of most players.

Simple tips

  • Early on, never draw the third side of a box.
  • Sacrifice small. When you must give something, open the shortest chain.
  • If your opponent does not know the double-cross, take what you are given but still use the trick yourself.
  • Count the safe lines left. If the number is against you, change it by giving away a box or two early: a small sacrifice that changes who runs out of safe moves is often the winning move.

Variations

  • Three or four players can play on a larger grid; alliances form quickly.
  • Triangles: use a triangular grid of dots and claim triangles.
  • Fewest boxes wins: the reverse game, which turns the strategy inside out.
