How to play mancala

Players
2
You need
A board with two rows of six pits and a store at each end, and 48 seeds, shells or pebbles
A game takes
10 to 20 minutes
Aim
Collect more seeds in your store than your opponent

Mancala is not one game but a huge family of "sowing" games played across Africa, the Middle East and Asia for a very long time. In all of them, players lift the seeds from a pit and drop them one by one into the pits that follow, as if sowing a field. India has its own members of the family, among them Pallanguzhi, played in Tamil Nadu on a board of fourteen pits with tamarind seeds or cowrie shells. The rules on this page are those of the simple two-row game with stores that is sold all over the world today. It is easy to learn, quick to play and quietly mathematical.

The board

Sit facing your opponent with the board lengthwise between you. The six pits nearest you are yours. The large pit at your right-hand end is your store. No board? Use an egg tray for twelve eggs and two bowls, or scoop twelve hollows in sand.

Setting up

Put four seeds in each of the twelve small pits. The stores start empty. (Three seeds a pit makes a shorter, easier game for small children; six makes a longer one.)

How to play

  1. On your turn, pick up all the seeds from any one of your six pits.
  2. Moving to the right (anticlockwise), drop one seed into each pit in turn, including your own store, and on round your opponent's pits if you have enough seeds. Skip your opponent's store.
  3. Extra turn: if your last seed falls in your own store, you play again.
  4. Capture: if your last seed falls in an empty pit on your own side, and the opponent's pit directly opposite has seeds in it, take that last seed and all the seeds opposite and put them in your store.
  5. Otherwise your turn ends.

You may not touch the seeds to count them before choosing a pit, by strict rules; at home, counting by eye is part of the fun.

The end

The game ends when all six pits on one side are empty. The other player takes the seeds remaining on their own side into their own store. Count the stores: whoever has more seeds wins. With 48 seeds, 25 or more is a win and 24 each is a draw.

Strategy

  • Look for the free turn. A pit holding exactly as many seeds as its distance from your store ends in the store. At the start, the third pit from your left holds four seeds and is four steps from the store: playing it gives you another turn at once. Chains of free turns are the engine of the game.
  • Empty your rightmost pit often. When it holds one seed, playing it is a free turn; then you can reuse it.
  • Set up captures. An empty pit on your side is a trap if you can land your last seed in it while the opposite pit is full. Count where each of your pits would end.
  • Watch their traps too. If your opponent has an empty pit opposite your fullest one, either move those seeds or make a move that spoils their count.
  • Big pits go round. A pit with thirteen seeds sows all the way round the board and ends in the pit it started from, now empty: a capture, if the pit opposite has seeds.
  • Think about the ending. Seeds left on your side when the game ends are yours. Late in the game, holding seeds back on your side while your opponent runs out of moves can be worth more than sowing them.
  • Going first is an advantage, so swap who starts from game to game.

Variations

  • Empty capture: some players allow the capture even when the opposite pit is empty, banking just the single seed.
  • No capture, no stores: many traditional games sow in laps, picking up the contents of the pit where the last seed lands and continuing. These are different games with their own rules.
  • Pallanguzhi and other Indian games use more pits and have rules about capturing fours and resting pits that vary from region to region. If an elder in your family knows one, ask to be taught; such games are best learnt from a player.

What it teaches

Counting, counting on, and thinking ahead: a child who plays mancala is doing mental arithmetic on every move without noticing. It is one of the best number games there is for ages six to ten.

Questions people ask

How many seeds go in each pit?

Four in the usual game, 48 in all. Use three for a shorter game.

Do I drop a seed in my opponent's store?

No. Skip it. You sow into your own store only.

What happens when one side is empty?

The game ends. The player who still has seeds on their side adds them to their own store, and the larger store wins.
