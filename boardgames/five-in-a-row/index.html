How to play five in a row (gomoku)

Players
2
You need
Squared paper and two pens, or a grid board and stones of two colours
A game takes
5 to 15 minutes
Aim
Be first to get five of your marks in an unbroken line

Five in a row is noughts and crosses grown up. The grid is large, the target is five instead of three, and suddenly there is real depth. The game is very old and is played across East Asia, where it is known by its Japanese name, gomoku, and played with black and white stones on the lines of a board. In school notebooks everywhere it is played with crosses and circles on squared paper. All you need is a pen each.

The board

A board of 15 by 15 is standard for the stone game; a board of 19 by 19, made for the game of Go, is also used. On squared paper there is no need to mark the edges, as long as there is plenty of room.

The rules

  1. The board starts empty. Black (or the player with crosses) moves first.
  2. Players take turns to place one stone, or draw one mark, on any empty place.
  3. Stones are never moved or removed.
  4. The first player to make an unbroken line of five of their own stones, across, down or diagonally, wins.
  5. If the board fills with no line of five, the game is a draw.

That is the whole game. One detail to agree on: does a line of six or more count? In the simple "free" game any line of five or more wins. In the traditional standard game a line must be exactly five; six in a row does not win.

The words players use

  • A four: four stones in a line with an empty place at one end. It threatens to become five next move and must be blocked at once.
  • An open four: four in a line with both ends empty. It cannot be stopped, because the opponent can block only one end. An open four wins.
  • An open three: three in a line with room at both ends. If it is not blocked, it becomes an open four next move. So an open three is also a threat that must be answered.

How games are won

Since a single four or a single open three can always be blocked, you win by making two threats with one stone. The classic winning shapes are:

  • Four and three: one stone completes a four and an open three together. The opponent must block the four, and the three then grows into an open four.
  • Double four: one stone makes two fours. Only one can be blocked.
  • Double three: one stone makes two open threes. The opponent can stop only one of them.

Good play is therefore about building stones that could become threats in several directions at once, and about spotting the same thing growing on the other side.

Tactics

  • Block open threes immediately, unless you can make a four of your own that forces a reply.
  • When you block, block on the side that helps you: choose the end where your stone also builds your own line.
  • Play in the middle. Lines near the edge have less room to grow.
  • Keep the initiative. Each move that forces a reply (a four, an open three) lets you choose where the fight goes. A string of forcing moves that ends in a double threat is how experts win; they call a winning sequence made only of fours a "victory by continuous fours".
  • Look diagonally. Beginners watch the rows and columns and lose on the diagonals.

Making it fair

The first player has a strong advantage; with perfect play on an unrestricted board, the first player can force a win. Ways to balance a friendly game:

  • Swap who starts each game, and play an even number of games.
  • The swap opening: one player places the first three stones (two black and one white) anywhere; the other then chooses which colour to play.
  • Restrictions on Black: in the tournament form called renju, the first player is forbidden to make a double three, a double four or a line of six. That is for enthusiasts.

For children

Start with noughts and crosses, move to "four in a row" on a six by six grid, then to five on open paper. The step from three to five teaches a child to look two moves ahead.
