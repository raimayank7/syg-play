How to play dominoes

Players
2 to 4
You need
A double-six set of 28 dominoes
A round takes
5 to 10 minutes
Aim
Play all your tiles first; score the spots left in the other hands

Dominoes are flat tiles divided into two halves, each half marked with a number of spots from none to six. Dozens of games can be played with them. This page explains the two simplest and most widely played, the block game and the draw game. They differ in only one rule, and between them they are what most people mean by "a game of dominoes". Rules for dominoes vary a great deal from place to place; where a point is commonly played in more than one way, we say so.

The set

The standard set is the double-six: 28 tiles showing every pair of numbers from blank-blank to six-six. Each number appears eight times in the set, a fact worth remembering. A tile with the same number on both halves is a double. The total number of spots on a tile is its weight: the six-six is the heaviest, the double blank the lightest.

Dealing

  1. Turn all the tiles face down and shuffle them by stirring them on the table.
  2. Each player draws a hand and stands the tiles on edge so that only they can see the faces. A common deal is seven tiles each for two players and five each for three or four. Many groups of four deal seven each, which uses the whole set.
  3. The tiles left over stay face down. They form the stock, often called the boneyard.

Who starts

The commonest rule: the player holding the highest double plays it to start the round. If nobody has a double, the heaviest tile starts, or the hands are drawn again. In later rounds, many groups let the winner of the previous round start with any tile.

Playing

  • Play passes to the left. On your turn, add one tile from your hand to either open end of the line. The half that touches must match the number at that end.
  • The line has two open ends. A double is laid crosswise, but in these games it is still just part of the line: play continues from its far side, not from its tips.
  • If you cannot play: in the block game, you pass. In the draw game, you draw tiles from the boneyard one at a time until you draw one you can play, and play it; if the boneyard runs out (many groups leave the last two tiles untouched), you pass.
  • You may not pass if you hold a tile that fits.

Ending a round

  • A round ends when a player lays their last tile. That player wins the round. Announcing it by saying "domino" is traditional.
  • A round also ends when it is blocked: no player can move. Everyone then counts the spots left in their hand, and the player with the lowest total wins the round.

Scoring

The winner of the round scores the total number of spots left in all the other players' hands. In a blocked round many groups score the difference between the hands instead. Play rounds until someone reaches an agreed target, commonly 100 points, or 50 for a short game. With four players you can play as two partnerships, partners sitting opposite and adding their scores.

Tips

  • Play your heavy tiles early. Spots left in your hand at the end count against you.
  • Play your doubles when you can. A double fits in only one way, so it is the tile most likely to be stranded.
  • Keep variety in your hand. If you hold tiles with many different numbers, you are less likely to be blocked.
  • Notice what others cannot play. When a player passes or draws, you learn which numbers they lack. Leave those numbers at the ends.
  • Count the sevens. Each number appears on seven different tiles (eight times, because the double carries it twice). If you can see six tiles of a number on the table and in your hand, only one more exists, and you may be able to block an end for good.
  • Make both ends the same number when you hold several tiles of that number and your opponents seem short of it.

Other games with the same tiles

  • Fives: you score during play whenever the open ends add up to a multiple of five.
  • Matching games for small children: lay all tiles face up and take turns to add any matching tile; first to finish wins.
  • Toppling: stand the tiles in a line and knock down the first. Not a game, but nobody with a set of dominoes has ever resisted it.

Good manners

Do not touch the boneyard except to draw, keep your tiles where others cannot see them, and place tiles gently: slamming them is a custom in some clubs and a nuisance in most living rooms.
