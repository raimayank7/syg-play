Draughts strategy: ten tips that win games

Draughts looks like a children's game and plays like a duel. Because capturing is compulsory, a good player can force the opponent's moves several turns ahead. These ten ideas are the difference between pushing discs and playing the game.

1. Use the compulsory capture as a weapon

Your opponent must take when a capture is offered. So you can give up one man on purpose to drag an enemy piece to a square where you capture two in return. Players call this a "shot". Before each move, ask: if I offer this man, where must their piece land, and what can I jump then? The simplest shot is the two-for-one, and games between beginners are usually decided by the first player to see one.

2. Hold the centre

A man in the middle of the board has two forward moves; a man on the edge has one. Pieces in the centre also take squares away from the enemy. Move towards the middle in the opening, and avoid drifting to the sides, where your men are safe from capture but do little.

3. Keep your back row as long as you can

The enemy cannot get a king while your men stand on your first row, because there is no empty square to land on. Do not hurry those men forward. The two that matter most are the ones that guard the squares between them; many players keep those two at home until late in the game and bring out the others.

4. Advance in pairs and triangles

A man with a friend diagonally behind it cannot be jumped, since the landing square is occupied. Move your men up together so that each is backed by another. A single man far ahead of the rest is a gift. Check, before moving a supporting piece, that you are not leaving the one in front undefended.

5. Do not leave holes behind your front line

An empty square directly behind one of your men, with an enemy piece in front, is an invitation to a jump. When you move a man out of your second or third row, look at what stood in front of it.

6. Exchange when you are ahead

With five men against four you have a small edge. With two against one you have a winning one. When you are a man up, trade one for one at every chance; the fewer pieces remain, the more your extra man matters. When you are behind, avoid trades and keep the position crowded.

7. Get the first king

A king moves backwards as well as forwards and can attack the enemy from behind, where plain men cannot answer. In an even game, the first king often decides it. Count how many moves each side needs to crown; if you win the race, run. If you would lose it, use the time to block or to force exchanges instead.

8. Use your king actively

Do not leave a new king sitting on the back row. Bring it out to chase lone enemy men, especially those on the edge, which have only one move and are easy to trap. But keep it away from groups of men that could surround it: a king is captured as easily as a man.

9. Count the moves

In a blocked position, the player who runs out of safe moves first must make a bad one. Before you lock up one side of the board, count how many free moves each player has elsewhere. Keeping a spare, harmless move in reserve is one of the quiet arts of the game.

10. Watch the double corner

Each side of the board has one corner where two dark squares touch the edges: the double corner. A lone king can shuffle between those squares and cannot be driven out by one enemy king. If you are losing, head for it and you may save a draw. If you are winning, keep the enemy out of it, or bring two kings to do the job.

A training game

Set up only four men each on your first rows and play. With so few pieces, every shot and every king race is easy to see, and a game takes two minutes. Ten of these teach more than one long game.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Moving the back-row men out at once, and letting the enemy crown early.
  • Lining men up along the edges "for safety".
  • Forgetting that a capture is forced, and so walking into a two-for-one.
  • Trading pieces when behind.
  • Chasing a king with a single king across open board: it cannot be caught that way.
