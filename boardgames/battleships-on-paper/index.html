How to play battleships on paper

Players
2
You need
Squared paper and a pen each, and something to hide your sheet behind
A game takes
15 to 30 minutes
Aim
Sink every ship in the other player's fleet

Battleships is a guessing game for two that needs nothing but paper. Each player secretly draws a fleet of ships on a grid, and the players take turns to call out squares, trying to find and sink the other's fleet. It was played with pencil and paper for decades before plastic versions were sold, and the paper game is still the best: you can choose your own grid, your own fleet and your own rules.

Drawing the grids

Each player draws two grids of ten by ten squares. Letter the columns A to J and number the rows 1 to 10, so that every square has a name such as C7. Label one grid "My fleet" and the other "My shots". Keep your sheet hidden behind a book.

The fleet

Agree on the fleet before you start. A common one has five ships:

ShipSquares
Aircraft carrier5
Battleship4
Cruiser3
Submarine3
Destroyer2

That is 17 squares of ship on a board of 100. Another popular fleet uses one ship of four squares, two of three, three of two and four of one. Any fleet works as long as both players have the same.

Placing the ships

  • Draw each ship on your "My fleet" grid as a straight row of squares, across or down. No diagonals, and no bending.
  • Ships may not overlap.
  • In many versions ships may not touch each other, even at a corner. Decide this before placing.
  • Once the game starts, ships cannot be moved.

Playing

  1. Decide who goes first. That player calls a square, for example "D4".
  2. The other player looks at their fleet grid and answers truthfully: "hit" if part of a ship is on that square, "miss" if not. They mark the shot on their fleet grid.
  3. The caller marks the result on their "My shots" grid: a cross for a hit, a dot for a miss.
  4. When every square of a ship has been hit, its owner says "sunk" and, in the usual rules, names the ship.
  5. Turns alternate, one shot each. (A common variation gives another shot after every hit.)
  6. The first player to sink the whole enemy fleet wins.

Honesty

The game depends on truthful answers. At the end, the players swap sheets and check. A ship that was moved, or a hit called as a miss, loses the game.

How to search well

  • Shoot in a chequerboard pattern. The smallest ship in the standard fleet covers two squares, so it cannot hide without touching a square of one colour of a chessboard. Calling only "black" squares finds every ship in at most fifty shots. Later, when only longer ships remain, widen the gaps: a ship of three cannot hide between shots three squares apart.
  • When you hit, hunt. Try the four squares next to the hit until you get a second hit; then you know which way the ship lies. Follow the line to both ends until it is sunk.
  • Use what "sunk" tells you. If ships may not touch, every square round a sunk ship is empty. Mark them as misses without wasting shots.
  • Think about what is left. If only the five-square ship remains, do not call a square where five in a row could not fit.
  • The middle is likelier than the corners. There are more ways for a ship to cover a central square than a corner square, so, against a player who places at random, central shots hit more often.

How to hide well

  • Do not line your ships up neatly or put them all on the edges; and do not always avoid the edges either. Be unpredictable from game to game.
  • If ships may touch, placing two side by side can fool a hunter into thinking it is one ship.
  • Spread ships out so that one lucky area of shots does not find two.

Variations

  • Salvo: on each turn you fire as many shots as you have ships still afloat, all called at once; the opponent then reports the hits. Faster, and losing ships hurts twice.
  • Smaller sea: a grid of six by six with three ships, for young children or a quick game.
  • Silent sinking: the owner says only "hit" or "miss" and never announces a sinking. Much harder.
  • Maths practice: number both sides of the grid and call squares as coordinate pairs, as in a graph. Teachers use this to practise reading coordinates.
