Carrom fouls and penalties

More carrom arguments are about fouls than about anything else. What happens when the striker goes down? What if it goes down along with a piece? What if you have nothing to pay the penalty with? This page sets out the usual fouls and their penalties in the standard game. Tournament laws go into finer detail, and families are entitled to their own rules, but these are the answers most clubs would give.

The basic penalty: a due

For almost every foul the penalty is the same:

  1. Your turn ends.
  2. One of your pieces that has already been pocketed is taken out and put back on the board. This returned piece is called a due, or penalty piece.
  3. Your opponent places it, anywhere inside the centre circle.

If you have not pocketed any piece yet, the due is owed. The next piece of yours that is pocketed, by anyone, is brought straight back out to pay the debt. You do not shoot again for pocketing a piece that goes to pay a due.

The fouls

The striker and an opponent's piece go down togetherFoul. The opponent's piece stays down (it helps them). You pay one due and lose the turn.
The striker and the queen go down togetherFoul. The queen returns to the centre, you pay one due and the turn ends.
The striker, or any piece, flies off the boardFoul. The piece is replaced in the centre; one due.

You shoot out of turnFoul. One due; anything pocketed comes back.
Your hand or arm crosses the diagonal line of your position, or your elbow comes over the boardFoul. One due.

Placing the striker

The striker must rest on your base lines touching both of them. At either end it must cover the red circle entirely or keep clear of it; half on the circle is not allowed. It must not touch the diagonal arrow line. If you notice a wrong placement before shooting, simply correct it. A shot played from a wrong placement is a foul.

The striker must leave the base lines when you shoot. If you flick and it fails to cross both lines, you are normally allowed to try again, up to three attempts, after which the turn is lost.

Shooting backwards

A piece lying on or behind your base line may not be struck directly: the striker must travel forward, across the front base line, before it touches anything. To reach a piece behind the line you must bounce the striker off a cushion first. In many homes thumb shots sent straight backwards are allowed; in the standard game the thumb may be used, but the striker must still go forward first.

Fouls with the queen

  • Queen pocketed but not covered: not a foul, but she comes back to the centre spot, placed by the opponent.
  • Queen pocketed and then an opponent's piece on the covering shot: the queen comes back; the opponent's piece stays down.
  • Pocketing your last piece while the queen is still on the board: in the professional rules you lose the board, and your opponent scores three points plus one for each of their pieces left.
  • Pocketing your opponent's last piece: you lose the board and three points.

Where returned pieces go

Dues and other returned pieces are placed flat inside the centre circle by the opponent of the player who fouled. A returned queen goes on the centre spot, or as near to it as possible if the spot is covered. The placer may put a due wherever in the circle suits them best, for example behind another piece.

Pieces that stand on edge or overlap

A piece that comes to rest on its edge, or leaning on another piece or on the striker, is left as it is until a shot moves it. If the striker comes to rest under a piece, lift the striker out carefully without disturbing the piece.

Friendly rules worth agreeing

  • Whether thumb shots are allowed at all.
  • Whether a player may stand for a difficult shot. In clubs, no.
  • How strictly "elbow on the board" is judged for children with short arms.
  • Whether, in the family points game, pocketing the striker costs a piece or a fixed number of points. Ten points is a common fine.

Keeping it pleasant

Call your own fouls. In club play the players themselves usually point out a touched piece or a pocketed striker before the opponent has to. A game in which every foul is disputed is no fun for anyone, and a child who sees adults own up learns more from that than from the game.
