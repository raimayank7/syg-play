How to play carrom: the rules

Players
2 (singles) or 4 (doubles, partners sit opposite)
You need
Carrom board, 9 white and 9 black pieces, 1 red queen, a striker, powder
A game takes
30 to 60 minutes
Aim
Pocket all your pieces first; play to 25 points

Carrom is the board game of the Indian courtyard and club: a smooth wooden square, four corner pockets, and small discs sent sliding by a flick of the finger. The game originated in India and is played all over South Asia. The rules below are those of the standard game as played in clubs and tournaments, simplified a little for home use. Many families play a "points" version instead; that and other variations are described at the end.

The equipment

  • Pieces: nineteen in all: nine white, nine black and one red, called the queen. They are also called carrom men or coins.
  • Striker: a larger, heavier disc that you flick. It is the only thing you touch.
  • Base lines: each side of the board has two parallel lines ending in a circle at each end. The striker is placed here to shoot.
  • Arrows: the diagonal lines pointing to each pocket.
  • Powder: a fine powder sprinkled on the board so that the pieces slide.

Setting up

Put the queen on the centre spot. Arrange six pieces round her in a ring, touching, with black and white alternating. Round those, a ring of the remaining twelve. The whole pack fits inside the centre circle. By custom the pack is turned so that white pieces point towards the player who breaks.

Who plays what

Toss for the break. The player who wins the toss breaks, that is, plays first, and plays white. The other player plays black. In doubles, partners sit opposite each other and share a colour, and turns pass round the board to the right.

How to shoot

  • Place the striker on your own base lines so that it touches both lines. If you place it at the end, it must cover the end circle completely or not touch the circle at all. It must not touch the diagonal arrow.
  • Flick the striker with a finger; do not push or carry it. Any finger, including the thumb, may be used.
  • Stay in your seat. Your hand may reach over the board but must not cross the diagonal lines at the sides of your position.
  • The striker may hit any piece, of either colour, directly or off the cushions.

Your turn

  • If you pocket one or more pieces of your own colour, you shoot again. You keep shooting for as long as you keep pocketing.
  • If you pocket nothing, the turn passes.
  • If you pocket one of your opponent's pieces, it stays down, to their benefit, and you lose your turn.

The queen

The red queen is the prize of every board, and she has her own rule: she must be covered.

  • You may pocket the queen at any time once play has begun.
  • Having pocketed her, you must pocket one of your own pieces on your very next shot. That covers the queen, and she is yours.
  • If you fail to cover, the queen is taken out and put back on the centre spot.
  • If the queen and one of your own pieces go down on the same shot, she is covered at once.
  • The queen cannot be left to the end: you may not pocket your last piece while she is still on the board. In the professional rules, pocketing your own last piece before the queen is covered loses you the board.

Fouls and "dues"

The commonest foul is pocketing the striker. The penalty for a foul is a due: one of your already pocketed pieces is taken out and placed in the centre circle by your opponent, and your turn ends. If you have no piece down yet, you owe one, and it is returned as soon as you pocket one. A piece pocketed on the same shot as the striker also comes back out. The full list is on fouls and penalties.

Winning a board

The round, called a board, ends when one player has pocketed all nine of their pieces, with the queen already covered by one side or the other. That player wins the board.

Scoring

  • The winner of the board scores one point for each of the opponent's pieces still on the board.
  • If the winner of the board also covered the queen, they add three points for her. As the rule is usually stated, the queen's points are no longer added once a player's score has reached 21.
  • If the loser covered the queen, nobody scores for her.
  • So a board is worth between 1 and 12 points.
  • The pieces are set up again and the other side breaks. The first to reach 25 points wins the game. Many clubs also limit a game to a fixed number of boards, with the leader winning if nobody has reached 25.

The family "points" game

Many homes do not use colours at all. Any player may pocket any piece, and each piece has a value: commonly 10 for a black, 20 for a white and 50 for the queen, or 5, 10 and 25. The queen must still be covered by a following piece. When the board is cleared, the player with the most points wins. It is simple, suits three players as well as two or four, and is a fine way to start; but it is a different game from the one played in clubs.

Who makes the official rules

The International Carrom Federation, formed in 1988 in Chennai, publishes the laws used in tournaments, and national bodies such as the All India Carrom Federation follow them. They fix details that a home guide leaves out, such as the size of the board (a playing surface of 74 cm square) and the exact procedure for every foul. For a competition, read the organiser's rules.

Questions people ask

How many pieces are there in carrom?

Nineteen, not counting the striker: nine white, nine black and one red queen.

What does covering the queen mean?

After pocketing the queen you must pocket one of your own pieces on your next shot. If you do, the queen is yours; if you miss, she goes back to the centre.

How many points is the queen worth?

Three points in the standard game, added to the score of the player who covered her, but only if that player also wins the board.

Is using the thumb allowed in carrom?

Yes. The International Carrom Federation allows a shot with any finger, including the thumb. Some families ban thumb shots as a house rule.

What happens if the striker goes into a pocket?

It is a foul. One of your pocketed pieces is returned to the centre of the board as a due, and you lose your turn.
