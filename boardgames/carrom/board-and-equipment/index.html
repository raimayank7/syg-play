Carrom board, pieces and striker: sizes and care

A carrom set lasts for decades and is handed down in many families. Whether you are buying your first board, replacing lost pieces or setting one up for a school or club, it helps to know what the standard sizes are and what makes a board play well. The measurements on this page are those given for the game as governed by the International Carrom Federation, as reported in the English Wikipedia article on carrom, read on 12 November 2026. A federation's own current rule book is the last word for tournament equipment.

The board

  • Playing surface: a square of 74 cm by 74 cm (about 29 inches). For competition the surface must measure between 73.5 and 74 cm on each side.
  • Frame: a raised wooden border round the playing surface, usually 5 to 10 cm wide, which gives the cushions their bounce.
  • Pockets: a round hole in each corner, with a net underneath to catch the pieces. A pocket is only slightly larger than a piece and smaller than the striker's path would suggest, which is what makes the game hard.
  • Markings: two parallel base lines with a circle at each end on all four sides, a diagonal arrow towards each pocket, and a centre circle with a spot for the queen.

Boards are sold in smaller sizes for children and for small homes. A "full size" or "tournament size" board is the 74 cm one; a board described by its outer measurement (for example 32 or 35 inches) includes the frame.

The pieces

There are nineteen pieces: nine white (or natural wood), nine black and one red queen. Approved pieces are plain discs with a rounded edge:

MeasureStandard
Diameter3.02 to 3.18 cm
Thickness7 to 9 mm
Weight5.0 to 5.5 g

Wooden pieces are standard. Plastic pieces are cheaper and lighter and slide differently; for anything more than a toy set, choose wood. Keep a few spares, because one always rolls under a cupboard.

The striker

The striker is a larger disc, normally about 4.1 cm across and about 15 grams in weight. Strikers are made of plastic, acrylic, bone-like resins or, traditionally, ivory substitutes; what matters is a smooth, round edge and a flat underside. Players become attached to their own striker, and in club play each brings one. A heavier striker hits harder and is harder to control; beginners do better with a standard one.

Powder

Carrom powder lets the pieces glide. Fine boric powder is the traditional choice in India; other fine powders made for the game are also sold. Use a small pinch, spread it with the palm of your hand or with a piece, and tip off the excess. Too much powder makes the board uneven and gets into the pockets. Keep powder away from small children's mouths and eyes, and wash hands after play.

What makes a good board

  • A smooth, flat playing surface. Good boards use thick, well-seasoned plywood with a fine finish. Run your palm over it: it should feel like glass, with no ridges at the joints of the markings.
  • Thickness. A thicker playing surface stays flat and gives a better rebound. Thin boards warp.
  • A solid, heavy frame. The frame should be hardwood, firmly joined at the corners. Knock a piece against the cushion: it should come back briskly. A dead cushion makes rebound shots impossible.
  • Clean pockets. The edges of the pocket should be smooth and the nets firmly fixed.
  • Accurate markings. Base lines straight and parallel, circles true.

Stand and seating

The board should lie flat and steady at a height where a seated player's forearm can rest on the frame. Clubs use a stand about the height of a low table, with a single lamp hung above the centre so that no player's hand casts a shadow. At home, a firm table or a stand works; a board balanced on a bed does not. Check with a marble or a piece that the board is level: the piece should not drift.

Looking after it

  • Keep it dry. Damp is the enemy; it warps the ply and swells the frame. Never wash the board with water.
  • Store it flat or hanging upright against a wall, never leaning at an angle for months.
  • Dust before play with a soft dry cloth, and clear old powder from the pockets.
  • Keep it out of direct sun, which dries and cracks the wood.
  • Do not sit or lean on the board, and keep heavy things off it.
  • Smooth the striker. A chipped striker scratches the surface. Replace it.

Sets for children

For children under about eight, a smaller board lets them reach the centre comfortably. They can move to a full-size board when their arms have grown. A full-size board is not too big for a keen child, but the far pockets will be out of range for a while.
