Carrom shots and tips: how to play better

Carrom is easy to play and hard to play well. The difference between a beginner and a club player is not strength; hard hitting is the beginner's mistake. It is a steady hand, a clear idea of where the striker will go after it hits, and patience. These are the things to practise.

How to hold and flick

  • Sit properly. Sit square to the board, close enough that your forearm can rest lightly on the frame. A steady arm gives a steady shot.
  • The straight grip. Rest your hand on the board with the fingertips touching the surface for support. Place the nail of the index finger (or middle finger) just behind the striker, touching it or almost touching it. Hold the finger back with the thumb, then release so that the finger snaps forward.
  • Do not swing. The finger should start touching the striker or within a hair's breadth of it. A finger that travels before it hits loses accuracy and may count as a push.
  • The scissor grip. For shots towards the side, many players hold the middle finger back with the index finger, like the blades of scissors, and flick sideways. Try both and keep what feels natural.
  • The thumb shot. For pieces near your own side, the thumb is flicked away from the palm. It is allowed in the standard game, but remember that the striker must still cross the front base line before touching a piece.

Aiming

For a straight shot, picture a line from the middle of the pocket through the middle of the piece and out the other side. The striker must hit the point on the piece where that line comes out. Place the striker on the base line so that pocket, piece and striker are in one line whenever you can; a straight shot is always the most reliable.

When they cannot be lined up, you need a cut: the striker hits the piece off-centre and the piece leaves at an angle. Aim the centre of the striker at an imaginary "ghost striker" touching the piece on the side exactly opposite the pocket. Thin cuts need a softer touch than you expect.

Control your strength

A piece hit gently drops into a pocket it would rattle out of at speed. Hit only hard enough for the piece to reach the pocket with a little to spare. Soft shots also keep the striker on the board: most pocketed strikers are the result of hitting too hard. The exception is the break, and shots meant to scatter a cluster.

The useful shots

  • Straight shot: striker, piece and pocket in line.
  • Cut shot: the piece is sent off at an angle.
  • Rebound (bank) shot: the striker hits a cushion first and comes back to strike a piece, the only fair way to reach a piece behind your base line. The striker leaves a cushion at about the same angle at which it arrived.
  • Double: the piece itself is sent against a cushion and rebounds into a pocket on your side.
  • Second-piece shot: the striker drives one piece into another, which goes down. Useful when your piece is hidden behind an opponent's.
  • Board shot: one stroke that pockets two pieces. Lovely when it works; do not count on it.

The break

The first shot should open the pack without giving the opponent easy pieces. A common plan is to place the striker a little to one side of the centre of your base line and hit the pack firmly at an angle, so that pieces spread towards the two far pockets and your white pieces, which are arranged to face you, get the best of it. Do not try to pocket the queen on the break.

Thinking a shot ahead

  • Take the easy pieces first? Not always. A piece sitting on the lip of a pocket will still be there later. Use your turn to pocket the awkward pieces while others are still on the board to help you, and leave the certainties for last.
  • Look where the striker will stop. You continue shooting after a pocket, but from your base line, so what matters is where the other pieces lie after the shot. Try not to knock your own easy pieces away.
  • Do not open pockets for the opponent. Before a hard shot, ask what you leave if you miss. A missed shot that scatters the opponent's pieces towards the pockets is worse than a safe, soft miss.
  • Block. When you cannot pocket, nudge one of your pieces in front of a pocket the opponent needs, or leave their pieces behind your own.

When to take the queen

The queen needs a cover on the very next shot, so pocket her only when one of your own pieces is in an easy position to follow. The best moment is in the middle of the board, when you still have several pieces near pockets. Do not leave her to the end: if your last piece and the queen remain, you must pocket the queen and then your last piece with no margin for error. And if your opponent is close to finishing, taking the queen becomes urgent, because a board cannot be won by someone whose last piece goes down while the queen is still out.

Practice drills

  1. Straight line: place one piece a hand's width from a far pocket and pocket it ten times running with the softest shot that works.
  2. The clock: place pieces in a circle round the centre and pocket each into the nearest pocket.
  3. Rebounds: place a piece behind your base line and pocket it off the far cushion.
  4. Striker control: shoot at a piece and try to make the striker stop inside the centre circle.

Looking after the board

A fast, even surface matters more than any technique. Use a little powder and spread it evenly; too much is as bad as none. Wipe the board with a dry cloth before play, keep it flat and away from damp, and keep the striker's edge smooth. See board and equipment.
