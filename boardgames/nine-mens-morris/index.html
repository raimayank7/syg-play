How to play nine men's morris

Players
2
You need
The board (easily drawn) and 9 counters each in two colours
A game takes
10 to 20 minutes
Aim
Leave the opponent with two pieces or no move

Nine men's morris is one of the oldest board games still played: it goes back at least to the Roman Empire, and boards have been found scratched into stone steps and temple floors in many countries. In India the same game is known by names such as Navakankari and Daadi. It needs only a board that can be drawn in a minute and eighteen counters, pebbles or seeds, which makes it a perfect travel and school-break game.

The board

The aim

Form a mill: three of your pieces in a straight row along one of the lines. Each time you form a mill, you remove one enemy piece. Reduce your opponent to two pieces, or leave them unable to move, and you win.

Phase 1: placing

  1. The board starts empty. Decide who goes first.
  2. Players take turns to place one piece on any empty point.
  3. If you complete a mill, remove one of your opponent's pieces from the board at once. It does not return.
  4. You may not remove a piece that is part of an enemy mill, unless every enemy piece on the board is in a mill.
  5. Placing continues until both players have placed all nine pieces.

Phase 2: moving

  1. Players now take turns to slide one piece along a line to the next point, if it is empty. No jumping.
  2. Forming a new mill again lets you remove an enemy piece, under the same rule about pieces in mills.
  3. A mill may be "opened" by moving one piece out and "closed" by moving it back on a later turn. Each time it closes, it counts as a new mill.
  4. A player who cannot move any piece loses.

Phase 3: flying

When a player is down to three pieces, those pieces may fly: on each turn one piece may jump to any empty point on the board, not just a neighbouring one. This gives the weaker side a fighting chance. Flying is treated as standard in many rule books and as an optional rule in others, so agree before you play.

Winning

You win when your opponent has only two pieces left, and so can never form a mill, or cannot make a legal move.

Tips

  • In the placing phase, think about movement, not mills. Beginners rush to make mills while placing and end up with pieces that cannot move. Spread out, and take points with many neighbours: the four mid-side points of the middle square each have four.
  • Avoid the corners early. A corner point has only two neighbours.
  • Block. When your opponent has two in a row with the third point empty, occupy it.
  • Build a double mill. Five pieces arranged so that one piece can slide back and forth between two mills, closing one each turn. The opponent loses a piece every move and has no time to answer. Spotting and preventing this "running mill" is the heart of the game.
  • Remove the piece that matters. Take the piece that blocks your mill or that would complete theirs, not the nearest one.

Smaller games

  • Three men's morris: a three by three grid of points, three pieces each. Place them in turn, then move one step along a line; the first row of three wins. A step up from noughts and crosses.
  • Six men's morris: two squares instead of three, six pieces each, no flying.
  • Twelve men's morris: the same board with diagonal lines added at the corners, twelve pieces each.

With perfect play by both sides the nine-piece game is a draw, but between people it is decided by who sees the double mill first. Print the board from printable boards.

Questions people ask

Can you remove a piece from a mill?

Only if the opponent has no pieces outside mills. Otherwise pieces in a mill are protected.

Can you open and close the same mill again and again?

Yes. Each time the mill is re-formed you remove another enemy piece, which is why a mill that the opponent cannot block is so strong.

What is flying?

When you have only three pieces left, you may move a piece to any empty point instead of only to a neighbouring one. Agree beforehand whether you play with this rule.
