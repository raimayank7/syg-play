Ludo house rules and variations

No two families play ludo quite alike, and every player is sure their own way is the real one. The truth is that ludo has a small set of core rules and a large set of local additions. None of the additions is wrong. Trouble starts only when players discover in the middle of a game that they grew up with different ones. Read this list aloud before the first roll and agree on each point; it takes two minutes.

Starting on a one as well as a six

With a six-only start, an unlucky player can sit out for many rounds. Allowing a one as well doubles the chance of getting out, from one roll in six to one in three, and makes the game friendlier for small children. A one does not give an extra roll.

Safe squares

With safe squares, a token on a star or on any start square cannot be captured, and tokens of different colours may share the square. The game becomes less savage and more tactical, since players plan their moves from star to star. Most printed Indian boards show eight safe squares: the four start squares and four stars. One point to settle: in some houses a token resting on another colour's start square can be captured by a token entering from that yard. Decide it before you begin.

Three sixes

The rule exists to stop one lucky player running away with the game. The mild form cancels only the third six. The harsh form undoes all the moves of that turn. The harshest sends the token that moved back to its yard. Choose the mild form for children.

Bonus roll for a capture or for reaching home

A very popular addition: you roll again after capturing a token, and again after bringing a token home. It rewards bold play and speeds the game up. Some houses give bonus squares instead of a bonus roll, for example moving any token twenty squares after a capture; that is rarer.

Blocks

Two of your tokens on one square make a block. Variations:

  • Soft block: the pair cannot be captured, but other tokens may jump over it.
  • Hard block: nobody may land on it or pass it. This makes blocks powerful, and a block in front of an opponent's home column entrance can hold them up for a long time. Some houses limit how many turns a block may stay.
  • Doubled tokens move together: the pair may move as one, by half the number rolled, on even rolls only. Rare, but it exists.
  • A block can be captured by a block: two tokens landing together (in two-dice play) may capture a pair.

"You must cut one before going home"

In this well-known Indian rule, a player may not enter the home column until they have captured at least one enemy token. A player who reaches the entrance without a capture must go round again. It forces everyone to fight and makes for a longer, livelier game. It can also be very hard on a child who never gets the chance to capture; consider waiving it for the youngest.

Two dice

With two dice, the two numbers are used separately, for the same token or for two different ones. A double usually gives another throw. In some versions either dice showing a six brings a token out. Two dice make the game faster and give more choice on every turn.

Partners

With four players, opposite colours can play as a team. Partners do not capture each other, and when one partner has all four tokens home, they use their rolls to move their partner's tokens. The team wins when all eight tokens are home.

A short game

If time is short, play with two tokens each, or agree that the first player to bring two tokens home wins.

Our suggestion for a family game

Start on a six or a one; safe squares on; extra roll for a six, for a capture and for reaching home; third six cancelled; soft blocks; no "must cut" rule; one dice; exact roll to finish. It is quick, gentle on beginners and still leaves room for skill. Whatever you choose, the rule that matters is the first one: agree before you start.
