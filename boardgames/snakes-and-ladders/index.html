How to play snakes and ladders

Players
2 or more
You need
A numbered board with snakes and ladders, one dice, a token each
A game takes
15 to 30 minutes
Aim
Be first to reach the last square

Snakes and ladders is usually the first board game a child learns. There are no choices to make: you roll, you move, and the board does the rest. That is exactly why it works for a four-year-old, and why adults can enjoy it as pure suspense. The game was born in India, where it began as a lesson about good and bad deeds.

The board

Most boards have 100 squares in ten rows of ten, numbered from 1 at the bottom left to 100 at the top. The numbering turns back at the end of each row, like a path winding up a hill. Ladders connect a lower square to a higher one. Snakes connect a higher square, where the head is, to a lower one, where the tail ends. Every board has its own arrangement.

The rules

  1. Each player chooses a token and places it beside square 1, off the board.
  2. Roll the dice to see who starts; the highest roll goes first, and turns pass to the left.
  3. On your turn, roll the dice and move your token forward that many squares, following the numbers.
  4. If you land on the foot of a ladder, climb at once to the square at its top.
  5. If you land on the head of a snake, slide at once down to the square at its tail.
  6. Landing on the top of a ladder or the tail of a snake does nothing; they work in one direction only. Passing over them does nothing either. Only the square you finish on matters.
  7. Two tokens may share a square. Nobody is captured or sent back.
  8. If you roll a six, you move and then roll again.
  9. The first player to reach the last square wins.

The two rules to agree on first

Do you need the exact number to finish?

Some families play that any roll that reaches or passes 100 wins. Many others require the exact number: from square 97 you need a three, and a larger roll means you stay where you are. A third version makes you "bounce back": from 97 a roll of five takes you three forward to 100 and two back to 98. The exact-roll versions make the ending tense, especially when a snake waits near the top. Choose one before you begin.

Do you need a six to start?

The simple rule is that everyone starts moving on their first roll. Some houses borrow the ludo rule and demand a six (or a one) before a token may enter the board. It adds nothing but waiting, and we suggest leaving it out for small children.

Other variations

  • Three sixes: a player who rolls three sixes in a row goes back to the start, or loses the turn.
  • Two tokens each: each player has two tokens and chooses which to move. This adds real decisions and suits older children.
  • Backwards game: start at 100 and race down to 1; now the snakes help and the ladders hurt.
  • Capture: landing on another player's token sends it back to the start. Lively, and likely to end in tears.

Where the game comes from

The game originated in ancient India, where it is known as Moksha Patam, and in a Jain form as Gyan Chaupar, the game of knowledge. The board was a picture of a moral journey: the ladders stood for virtues such as generosity and faith that lift a person up, the snakes for vices such as anger and greed that drag one down, and the last square for liberation. On the old boards the snakes outnumbered the ladders, to teach that the path of goodness is harder than the path of wrong.

The game was taken to Britain in the 1890s, where the earliest known English version was registered in 1892 and the moral names gradually disappeared. In the United States a version with playground slides in place of snakes, called Chutes and Ladders, was published in 1943. The board came back to Indian homes in its simplified form, printed on the reverse of the ludo board.

What children learn from it

  • Counting on: moving a token one square per count, without counting the square they start on.
  • Numbers to 100 and their order, including reading the row that runs backwards.
  • Taking turns and waiting.
  • Losing well. Because the game is pure chance, a snake on square 98 can happen to anyone, and the youngest player has the same chance as the oldest. It is a gentle place to practise disappointment.

Is there any skill?

None at all in the standard game: the player has no decisions. Mathematicians like the game for that reason, as a tidy example of a process ruled only by chance, in which the length of a game can be calculated for any given board. If your family wants choices, play with two tokens each.

Questions people ask

What happens if you land on the bottom of a snake?

Nothing. A snake only works from its head down to its tail. You stay on the square.

Do you get another turn for a six in snakes and ladders?

Yes, in the usual rules: move the six, then roll again.

Can two players be on the same square?

Yes. In the standard game tokens share squares freely and nobody is sent back.

Is snakes and ladders an Indian game?

Yes. It originated in ancient India as Moksha Patam, a game about virtues and vices, and reached Britain in the 1890s.
