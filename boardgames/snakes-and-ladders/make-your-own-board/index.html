Make your own snakes and ladders board

A home-made snakes and ladders board is one of the best rainy-day projects there is. It needs a sheet of chart paper, a ruler and felt pens. Children who design a board understand the game far better than those who only play it, and a teacher can turn the board into a lesson on almost any subject. Here is how to make one that plays well.

What you need

  • A large sheet of paper or card. Chart paper is ideal; the back of an old calendar works.
  • A ruler and pencil, then pens or crayons.
  • One dice, or our dice roller.
  • A token for each player: buttons, coins, bottle caps or pebbles.

Step 1: draw the grid

For a full game, draw a square grid of ten rows and ten columns. On chart paper, squares of 5 cm give a board 50 cm across, big enough for tokens and small hands. For young children or a short game, a board of 6 by 6 (36 squares) or 8 by 8 (64 squares) is plenty.

Step 2: number the squares

Start with 1 in the bottom left corner and number along the bottom row to the right. At the end of the row go up one square and number the next row from right to left. Keep winding back and forth to the top. On a ten by ten board, 100 ends up in the top left corner. This zigzag is what makes moving along the board easy to follow.

Step 3: place the ladders and snakes

This is the design part, and a few guidelines make the difference between a good board and a frustrating one.

  • How many? On 100 squares, six to nine ladders and six to nine snakes play well. Our printable board has six of each. Fewer makes a plain dice race; many more makes the game endless.
  • Balance them. Add up the squares gained by all the ladders and the squares lost by all the snakes. If the totals are roughly equal, the game takes a reasonable time. More snake than ladder makes a long game; the old Indian boards did this on purpose.
  • Vary the lengths. Include one long ladder and one long snake for drama, and several short ones. A snake from the nineties down to the teens is the square everyone will remember.
  • Guard the finish. Put a snake's head in the last row, a few squares before the end. It keeps the leader nervous.
  • Never start two things on one square. A square can hold the foot of one ladder or the head of one snake, not both, and not two of either.
  • Do not chain them. The top of a ladder should not be the head of a snake, and a snake's tail should not be the foot of a ladder, unless you want a chain reaction on purpose.
  • Leave the first and last squares clear. No ladder from square 1 and nothing on the final square.
  • A ladder always goes up, a snake always goes down. The head of the snake is on the higher number.

Step 4: test it

Play two or three games before you colour everything in. If games drag, shorten a snake or add a ladder. If someone wins in six turns every time, do the reverse.

Themes that teach

The original Indian game was a lesson in itself: ladders stood for good deeds and snakes for bad ones. The same idea makes a fine classroom or family project.

  • Good habits. Write a habit at the foot of each ladder (brushed teeth, helped at home, read a book) and a bad one at each snake's head (too much screen time, told a lie).
  • Health and safety. Washing hands and wearing a helmet are ladders; skipping breakfast and crossing the road without looking are snakes.
  • Environment. Planting a tree, saving water and switching off lights against littering and wasting food.
  • Any school subject. To climb a ladder a player must first answer a question correctly: a multiplication fact, a spelling, a capital city. A wrong answer at a snake's head sends the player down; a right one saves them.
  • History or a story. Squares follow the events of a story or a freedom struggle; helpful events are ladders, setbacks are snakes.

Other shapes of game

  • Giant floor board. Chalk a 5 by 5 grid on a courtyard or terrace, and let the children be the tokens. Use a large cardboard dice.
  • Mini travel board. A 6 by 6 board on a postcard with a coin for a dice: heads moves one, tails moves two.
  • Maths version for older children. Use two dice and let the player choose to add or subtract them. Now there are decisions to make, and a clever player can dodge a snake.

Rules to write on the board

Write your house rules in a corner so that nobody argues later: whether an exact roll is needed to finish, and whether a six gives another turn. The standard rules are on how to play snakes and ladders.
