How the chess pieces move

There are six kinds of chess piece and each moves in its own way. In the diagrams the dots show every square the piece could move to on an empty board. Two rules hold for all of them: a piece cannot move onto a square occupied by one of its own side, and it captures by moving onto a square occupied by an enemy piece, which is then removed.

The rook

The rook moves in straight lines: forwards, backwards or sideways, as many squares as are free. It cannot jump, so it stops in front of a friendly piece, or captures the first enemy piece in its path. Rooks are strongest on open files, columns with no pawns in the way. Each side starts with two, in the corners.

The bishop

The bishop moves diagonally, as far as the way is clear. Because it only moves on diagonals, a bishop stays on squares of one colour for the whole game. Each side starts with one bishop on light squares and one on dark squares.

The queen

The queen is the most powerful piece. She combines the rook and the bishop: any number of free squares in a straight line or along a diagonal. From the centre of an empty board she can reach 27 squares. She cannot jump, and she cannot make the knight's move.

The king

The king moves one square in any direction. He may not move to a square that is attacked by an enemy piece, since that would put him in check. For the same reason the two kings can never stand next to each other. Once in the game the king may make a special double move with a rook, called castling; see special moves.

The knight

The knight's move has the shape of a capital L: two squares in a straight line, then one square to the side. It always lands on a square of the opposite colour to the one it left. The knight is the only piece that can jump over other pieces, friend or enemy; only the square where it lands matters. From the centre it reaches eight squares, from a corner only two, which is why players say "a knight on the rim is dim".

The pawn

The pawn is the humblest piece and has the most rules.

  • It moves straight forward one square, if that square is empty.
  • On its first move only, a pawn may advance two squares instead of one, if both squares are empty.
  • It captures differently from how it moves: one square diagonally forward, to the left or right. A pawn cannot capture the piece directly in front of it, and so two pawns that meet head to head block each other.
  • It never moves or captures backwards.
  • A pawn that reaches the far end of the board is promoted to a stronger piece, and there is a special capture called en passant. Both are on special moves.

Mistakes beginners make

  • Moving the knight wrongly. Count "two, then one" and check that the colour of the square has changed.
  • Capturing straight ahead with a pawn. Pawns take diagonally only.
  • Moving a pawn two squares later in the game. The double step is allowed only from the pawn's starting square.
  • Jumping with a bishop, rook or queen. Only the knight jumps.
  • Moving the king next to the other king, or onto any attacked square. It is not allowed, and if it happens the move must be taken back.

A simple way to practise

Put one piece alone on the board and choose a far square. Count the fewest moves it needs to get there. A rook reaches any square in at most two moves; a bishop reaches any square of its own colour in at most two; a knight may need as many as six. Then play "pawn wars": set up only the kings and pawns, and the first player to promote a pawn wins. It teaches the pawn rules faster than any explanation.
