Chess notation: how to read and write moves

Chess moves can be written down in a short code, so that a game can be recorded, replayed and studied. Every chess book, newspaper column and app uses the same system, called algebraic notation. It takes ten minutes to learn, and it opens the door to the whole literature of the game.

Naming the squares

Each square has a name made of a letter and a number. The files (columns) are lettered a to h from White's left to White's right. The ranks (rows) are numbered 1 to 8 starting from White's side. The white king starts on e1, the black king on e8. Always read the letter first: e4 is the e-file, fourth rank.

Writing a move

Write the letter of the piece, then the square it moves to.

  • Nf3: a knight moves to f3.
  • Bc4: a bishop moves to c4.
  • e4: a pawn moves to e4. Pawn moves have no letter, only the square.

When two pieces could make the same move

If both of your knights could go to d2, writing Nd2 is not enough. Add the file the knight comes from: Nbd2 is the knight from the b-file. If the two pieces stand on the same file, use the rank instead: R1e2 is the rook from the first rank.

Numbering the moves

A game is written in numbered pairs: White's move, then Black's. So "1. e4 e5" means White played the pawn to e4 and Black answered with the pawn to e5. When a line of text starts with Black's move, three dots mark the gap: "1... e5".

A whole game in notation

Here is the four-move checkmate that catches many beginners:

1. e4 e5   2. Bc4 Nc6   3. Qh5 Nf6   4. Qxf7#

Read it aloud: pawn to e4, pawn to e5; bishop to c4, knight to c6; queen to h5, knight to f6; queen takes on f7, checkmate. Set up a board and play it through. The final position looks like this:

Comments in books

Writers add marks after a move to give their opinion: ! a good move, !! a brilliant one, ? a mistake, ?? a blunder, !? interesting, ?! doubtful. They are opinions, not part of the move.

Why bother writing your games?

  • You can replay a game afterwards and find where it turned. This is the quickest way to improve.
  • In a tournament, the score sheet is the evidence if a dispute arises, for example a claim of threefold repetition.
  • You can follow games printed in books and newspapers, including those of the world champions.

Start by recording just your first ten moves in each game. Within a week the square names will come without counting.

The older system

Old books in English use a different, descriptive notation, with moves such as "P-K4" (pawn to king's fourth). It names squares from each player's own side, so every square has two names. You only need to recognise it if you pick up a book printed before about 1980; everything today uses the algebraic system described here.
