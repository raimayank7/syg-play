Opening principles and basic strategy

Knowing how the pieces move is not the same as knowing what to do with them. Beginners often push pawns at random or send the queen out alone and wonder why they lose. You do not need to memorise openings. A handful of principles, followed patiently, will beat most players who ignore them.

The first ten moves: three aims

1. Take a share of the centre

The four squares in the middle of the board, d4, d5, e4 and e5, are the high ground. A piece in the centre reaches more squares and can swing to either side. Begin by moving one or both centre pawns two squares, so that your pawns hold central squares and your bishops and queen have lines to come out on.

2. Bring out your pieces

Knights and bishops do nothing on the back rank. In the opening, each move should bring a new piece into play. Knights usually go to the squares in front of the bishops (f3 and c3 for White, f6 and c6 for Black); bishops to open diagonals. Good habits:

  • Knights before bishops, as a rule of thumb: it is clearer where knights belong.
  • Do not move the same piece twice in the opening unless you must.
  • Do not bring the queen out early. The enemy will chase her with smaller pieces, bringing them into play with every attack while you lose time.
  • Do not make pawn moves that do not help your pieces. Every pawn move is permanent.

3. Castle

A king in the middle, on an open file, is a target. Castle within the first ten moves if you can, usually on the short side. It puts the king behind a wall of pawns and joins the rooks. After castling, do not push the three pawns in front of your king without a strong reason.

The habit that matters most: look before you move

Most beginners' games are decided by a piece left where it can be taken for nothing. Before every move, ask two questions:

  1. What did my opponent's last move threaten? Look at what the moved piece now attacks, and at what it has uncovered behind it.
  2. After my move, can anything of mine be captured? Check the square you are moving to, and the pieces your move leaves behind.

This takes ten seconds and wins more games than any opening.

Counting attackers and defenders

Before capturing on a square, count how many of your pieces attack it and how many enemy pieces defend it. If you have more attackers than there are defenders, you will win something, provided you capture with your least valuable piece first. Use the scale: pawn 1, knight and bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9. Do not give a rook for a knight, or a queen for a rook, without a clear reason.

Simple tactics to look for

  • The fork. One piece attacks two enemy pieces at once; only one can be saved. Knights are the great forkers, but a pawn or queen can fork too.
  • The pin. A piece cannot move because a more valuable piece stands behind it on the same line. Pile attackers on a pinned piece.
  • The skewer. The reverse of a pin: a valuable piece is attacked, and when it moves, the piece behind it is taken.
  • The discovered attack. Moving one piece uncovers an attack from another behind it. If the piece that moves also makes a threat, the opponent faces two threats at once.

When you are ahead

If you have won a piece, exchange pieces of equal value whenever you can. With fewer pieces on the board, your extra one counts for more, and the enemy has fewer chances to surprise you. Keep your pawns: one of them will become a queen. Then finish with one of the basic checkmates.

When you are behind

Avoid exchanges, keep the position complicated, and look for chances of stalemate or perpetual check. Never resign in a beginner's game: your opponent may not know how to finish.

Traps to know

  • The four-move mate on f7. Queen and bishop aim at the pawn beside your king. Block or guard before it lands; see the game written out.
  • The loose bishop or knight. A piece moved to the edge with no defender is often picked off by a queen check that attacks it at the same time. Keep your pieces protecting one another.
  • The back rank. A castled king behind three unmoved pawns can be mated by a rook or queen arriving on the back rank. When things are quiet, give your king an escape square by moving one of those pawns up one step.

How to improve

Play often, against people slightly better than you. Write down your games and look for the move where you lost a piece. Solve a few simple "mate in one" and "win a piece" puzzles each day. And when you lose, shake hands; every strong player has lost thousands of games.
