Basic checkmates every player should know

Winning material is only half of chess. Many beginners find themselves a queen ahead and unable to finish, chasing the enemy king round the board until the game is drawn. Three simple methods cure this for good. Practise each against a friend, or against yourself, until you can do it without thinking.

The one idea behind all three

A lone king cannot be checkmated in the middle of the board; there are too many squares to cover. He must be driven to an edge, where the edge itself takes away three of his escape squares. Every method below is a way of shrinking the king's space until he stands on an edge, and then giving the final check.

1. Two rooks: the ladder

This is the easiest checkmate and needs no help from your own king. One rook guards a rank so that the enemy king cannot cross it. The other rook checks on the next rank, forcing the king back. Then the first rook steps past and checks on the rank beyond. The rooks climb like feet on a ladder until the king is on the last rank.

If the enemy king walks towards one of your rooks to attack it, simply slide that rook to the far end of its rank and carry on. A queen and a rook, or two queens, mate in the same way.

2. King and queen against king

The queen can drive the king to the edge by herself, but she needs her own king beside her to deliver mate.

  1. Box him in. Place the queen a knight's move away from the enemy king. He is not in check, but his space is cut down. Each time he moves, move the queen the same way, keeping the knight's-move distance. The box shrinks until he is on the edge.
  2. Stop and check for stalemate. When the king is confined to the edge with one or two squares to shuffle between, leave the queen where she is.
  3. Bring your king. Walk your king up until he stands opposite the enemy king with one square between them, or guards the square where the queen will land.
  4. Mate. Put the queen next to the enemy king, protected by your own king.

The danger is stalemate. If the enemy king is in a corner and your queen sits a knight's move away, he has no move at all, and if he is not in check the game is drawn. Always leave him a square to move to until the mating move itself.

3. King and rook against king

This takes longer, because the rook cannot guard the squares next to itself. King and rook must work together.

  1. Cut the board. Put the rook on a rank or file that the enemy king cannot cross. He is now shut in one part of the board.
  2. Bring your king up to face the enemy king.
  3. Wait for the kings to stand opposite each other with one square between them, on the side of the rook's line where the enemy king is. When that happens, a rook check along the line forces him back one row, because your king covers the three squares in front of him.
  4. Move the wall up and repeat. If the kings are not opposite and it is your move, make a waiting move with the rook along its line; the enemy king must then step into opposition or retreat by himself.
  5. On the edge, the same check is checkmate.

The method always works within the fifty moves that the rules allow, with room to spare, but only if you do not give pointless checks. A check that does not force the king backwards just lets him run.

What cannot win

A king with a single bishop, or a single knight, cannot checkmate a lone king, and the game is drawn at once. Two bishops can force mate; a bishop and a knight can too, though the method is hard and even strong players have to study it. Two knights cannot force it against good defence. So when you are ahead, do not trade down to a single minor piece: keep a pawn that can become a queen.

How to practise

Set up the three positions with the enemy king in the centre and play them out, taking the defender's side in turn with a friend. Count your moves. When you can give mate with the queen in under fifteen moves and with the rook in under twenty-five, every time, you will never again throw away a won game.
