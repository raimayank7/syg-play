Check, checkmate, stalemate and draws

A game of chess ends in a win for one side or in a draw. This page explains every ending, starting with the idea on which the whole game rests: check.

Check

A king is in check when an enemy piece attacks it, meaning the piece could capture the king on its next move. You are not allowed to leave your king in check. There are exactly three ways to answer a check:

  1. Move the king to a square that is not attacked.
  2. Capture the piece that is giving check.
  3. Block the check by putting one of your pieces between the attacker and the king. This cannot be done against a knight, which jumps, or against a piece standing right next to the king.

If two pieces give check at once (a double check), only the first way works: the king must move.

You may also never make a move that exposes your own king to check, for example by moving a piece that was shielding him. Such a piece is said to be pinned.

Checkmate

If the king is in check and none of the three answers is possible, it is checkmate, and the game is over. The side that gave checkmate wins.

Other ways to win

  • Resignation. A player who sees that the position is lost may give up at any time. Among experienced players most games end this way.
  • Time. In games played with a clock, a player whose time runs out loses, provided the opponent still has enough pieces to give checkmate in some way.

Stalemate

Stalemate happens when the player whose turn it is has no legal move and is not in check. The game is a draw at once. It is the great trap for the side that is winning: with a queen against a lone king it is easy to take away every square without giving check.

To avoid it, before each move that tightens the net, ask: "After this, does the other king have a move, or am I giving check?" If neither, choose another move.

All the kinds of draw

  1. Stalemate, as above.
  2. Agreement. The players may agree to a draw at any time.
  3. Threefold repetition. If exactly the same position, with the same player to move and the same possible moves, occurs three times in a game, a player may claim a draw. The commonest case is perpetual check, where one side checks for ever and the other cannot escape.
  4. The fifty-move rule. If fifty moves by each side pass with no capture and no pawn move, a player may claim a draw. The rule stops a game from going on for ever when nobody is making progress.
  5. Not enough material. If neither side has the pieces to give checkmate by any series of moves, the game is drawn. King against king; king and one bishop against king; king and one knight against king: none of these can ever checkmate.

In competition there are stricter versions of rules 3 and 4, under which the game is declared drawn automatically after five repetitions or seventy-five moves; the laws of the world chess federation, FIDE, give the exact wording.

How results are scored

In tournaments a win counts one point, a draw half a point and a loss nothing. That is why a result is written 1-0 (White won), 0-1 (Black won) or ½-½ (drawn).

Common questions at the board

  • "You did not say check, so it does not count." Wrong. Check exists whether or not anyone announces it.
  • "My king was in check and I did not notice; we played on." The position must go back to the move where the check was ignored, and a legal move be played instead.
  • "I have only a king left, so I lose." Not until you are checkmated. A lone king can still draw by stalemate or by the fifty-move rule.
  • "The king reached the other side, so I get a piece back." There is no such rule. Only pawns promote.
