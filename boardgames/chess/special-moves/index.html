Special moves: castling, en passant and promotion

Three moves in chess break the normal pattern. Beginners often learn them wrongly, or meet them for the first time when an opponent plays one and an argument follows. Here is each one in full.

Castling

Castling is the only move in which two pieces move at once, and the only time the king moves two squares. Its purpose is to put the king in a safer place behind pawns and to bring a rook towards the centre.

How to do it: move the king two squares towards a rook, then lift that rook over the king and set it on the square the king has just crossed. Castling on the side nearer the king is called castling short, or kingside; on the other side, castling long, or queenside.

Castling is allowed only if all of these are true:

  1. The king has not moved earlier in the game.
  2. The rook you are castling with has not moved earlier in the game.
  3. Every square between the king and that rook is empty.
  4. The king is not in check at the moment.
  5. The king does not pass over a square attacked by an enemy piece.
  6. The king does not land on an attacked square.

Points people get wrong: you may castle even if the rook is attacked, and in castling long the rook may pass over an attacked square (b1 or b8); only the king's path matters. Having been in check earlier in the game does not stop you castling later, provided the king did not move. Touch the king first when you castle; under the touch-move rule, a player who touches the rook first may be made to move only the rook.

En passant

En passant is French for "in passing". It is a special way for a pawn to capture a pawn.

The situation: your pawn stands on its fifth rank (rank 5 for White, rank 4 for Black). An enemy pawn on the next file uses its double first step and lands right beside your pawn. On your very next move, and only then, you may capture it as if it had advanced just one square: your pawn moves diagonally forward to the square the enemy pawn skipped over, and the enemy pawn is removed.

Why does the rule exist? The double step was added to speed up the opening. Without en passant, a pawn could use the double step to slip past an enemy pawn that was waiting to capture it. The rule keeps that chance alive for one move.

  • Only pawns can capture en passant, and only pawns can be captured that way.
  • The right lasts for one move. If you play something else, it is gone.
  • It is never compulsory, unless it happens to be your only legal move.

Promotion

When a pawn reaches the last rank, the far edge of the board, it must be replaced at once, as part of the same move, by a queen, rook, bishop or knight of its own colour. The choice is yours. It cannot stay a pawn and it cannot become a king.

  • Almost always the choice is a queen, the strongest piece. Players call this "queening".
  • You are not limited to pieces that have been captured. You may have two queens, or three, or nine. If no spare queen is to hand, an upturned rook is the usual stand-in at home.
  • Choosing a piece other than a queen is called underpromotion. It is rare but sometimes right: a knight may give a check that a queen could not, and a rook is occasionally chosen to avoid giving stalemate.
  • The new piece acts immediately: it can give check or checkmate on the move it appears.

Promotion is why pawns matter so much late in the game. A single extra pawn that cannot be stopped becomes a queen, and a queen usually decides everything.

Quick test

Can you castle if your king was checked ten moves ago but never moved? Yes. Can you capture en passant two moves after the enemy pawn passed? No. Can you promote to a second queen while your first is still on the board? Yes. If any of these surprised you, read the section again; these three answers settle most family arguments about chess.
