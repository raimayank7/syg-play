Café wall illusion

Rows of black and white tiles separated by grey lines, each row shifted along from the one above. Do the grey lines look level, or tilted like wedges?

The test

Press the button to draw red horizontal lines through the grey lines. They are exactly horizontal: the drawing places each of the 8 rows of tiles at an even distance. The grey lines were never tilted.

Where it comes from

The psychologist Richard Gregory noticed it in 1973 on the tiled wall of a café in Bristol, England, and the name stuck. Gregory and a colleague, Priscilla Heard, published a study of it in 1979. Tile patterns that cause the same effect are found in buildings around the world.

Why it happens

The effect needs the grey mortar line, which must be lighter than the black tiles and darker than the white ones. Each black tile next to the line makes the line look as though it tilts one way, and the shifted next tile makes it tilt the other way, so the line seems to be a long thin wedge. Scientists think that early parts of the visual system, which detect edges in tiny patches, combine these small slopes in a way that is wrong for the whole picture.

Try this

Try covering one end of the picture so you see only a few tiles: the effect gets weaker. Try squinting. A designer's lesson: if the mortar is the same colour as the white tiles or the black tiles, the illusion goes, so artists use grey mortar to get the effect on purpose.

Question

Why does it work only with the grey line?

The line must be a middle brightness. Without it the tiles just look like a checker pattern, and the shifted rows give no sign of tilt.
