Zöllner illusion

Four long horizontal lines crossed by short slanting strokes that lean in opposite directions on neighbouring lines. Do the long lines look parallel?

The test

Press the button to hide the short strokes. The long lines were drawn exactly 50 units apart over their whole length, so they are perfectly parallel: the tilt is added by the strokes.

Where it comes from

Johann Karl Friedrich Zöllner, a German astrophysicist, noticed the illusion in 1860 on a fabric pattern. It was one of the first to be studied in a laboratory and became a standard example of a geometric illusion.

Why it happens

Short strokes at an angle to a long line push the long line's apparent direction away from the stroke direction. That is an exaggeration of small angles of the same kind as in the Poggendorff illusion, which is why the two are often explained together. Opposite strokes on neighbouring lines make the lines tilt in opposite ways, which makes them look as if they converge or diverge.

Try this

Turn the page so that you view it at 45 degrees, where the effect is often strongest or weaker: try it and see. Hold a ruler on the screen to check the lines are the same distance apart at each end. A challenge: draw your own set on paper using a ruler and a set square.

Question

Why do the lines look tilted?

Because the short strokes make the long lines look as if they lean away from them. The lines themselves are straight and parallel.
