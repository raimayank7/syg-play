Poggendorff illusion

A slanted line goes behind a wide grey bar and comes out the other side. Does it line up with where it went in?

The test

Press the button to draw the true straight line. The two black pieces lie on it exactly: both have the same slope of -1.75 (the drawing computes the right piece from the same line formula as the left piece).

Where it comes from

Johann Christian Poggendorff, a German physicist, described the effect in 1860 after receiving a drawing from the astronomer Johann Zöllner, who had noticed it. It appears in old buildings and is used by architects as a warning about long lines crossing wide bars.

Why it happens

The usual idea is that we exaggerate small angles: the acute angle between the slanted line and the bar edge looks bigger than it is, which pushes the line out of place on one side. The effect is smaller when the line is vertical or horizontal, and larger when the bar is wide. Other researchers add that the visual system tries to join lines across the bar as if they were in 3D space.

Try this

Lay a ruler on the screen or a straight edge of paper across the two line pieces and see if it touches both. Make the bar narrower by turning the page: the effect gets weaker. Try drawing your own version and see how far apart a gap needs to be before it fools you.

Question

Does it work with a vertical line?

The effect is much weaker with a vertical or horizontal line, and strongest with lines at about 20 to 30 degrees from the bar.
