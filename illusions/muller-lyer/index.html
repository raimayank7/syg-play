Müller-Lyer illusion

Two horizontal lines. The top one ends in arrowheads (the fins point back towards the line) and the bottom one ends in tails (the fins point away). Which line is longer?

The test

Press the button to draw two dashed red lines straight down from the ends of the top line. They also touch the ends of the bottom line: both lines are exactly 200 units long, because the drawing is made from one number used for both.

Where it comes from

The illusion is named after Franz Carl Müller-Lyer, a German sociologist who published it in 1889. It is one of the most studied illusions in psychology, and it is nearly always the first one in a textbook.

Why it happens

There is no single accepted explanation. One idea, the "carpentered world" idea, says that we read the fins as corners of rooms or buildings, and a line that looks farther away gets stretched in our judgement. Another says our eyes simply average the whole figure, fins included, so the line with tails gets a longer total extent. Studies from the 1960s found that people in some societies without many straight-edged buildings were fooled less, though later work argued about what that proves.

Try this

Cover the fins with two fingers and look at the lines alone: they look equal at once. Ask a friend which line looks longer before you show the red guides; almost everyone says the one with tails. Then try the drawing on paper with a ruler and see how large the effect is for you: for many people it is about 20 per cent.

Question

Why does the top line look shorter?

Because of its arrowhead fins. The line itself is exactly the same length as the other.
