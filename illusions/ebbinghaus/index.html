Ebbinghaus illusion

Two orange circles. One sits in a ring of big grey circles and the other in a ring of small ones. Which orange circle is bigger?

The test

Press the button to draw two black outlines of exactly the same radius around the two orange circles. They fit both: each orange circle has a radius of 22 units, from one number in the drawing.

Where it comes from

It is named after Hermann Ebbinghaus, the German psychologist best known for his work on memory, and is also called the Titchener circles after Edward Titchener, who put it in a textbook in 1901. It is used in research about how the brain judges size from context.

Why it happens

We rarely judge size alone; we judge it against what surrounds an object. Big neighbours make a circle look small and small neighbours make it look larger. Some researchers think the illusion comes from comparing the circle with the gap left by the surrounding circles, others that it comes from a mix of size contrast and how close the neighbours are. The strength changes with how near the circles are and how many there are.

Try this

Hold a card over the surrounding circles and compare the orange circles alone. Move the card away and they seem to change size again. A fun fact researchers use: some studies found that grasping the circle with the hand is less fooled than judging by eye, though that result is still debated.

Question

Is it true that grabbing the circle ignores the illusion?

Some studies found a smaller effect on grasping than on judging by eye, but later studies disagreed, so there is no settled answer.
