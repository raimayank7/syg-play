Hermann grid illusion

A grid of black squares with white lines between them. Look at the white crossings. Do you see little grey smudges? Now look right at one: it vanishes.

The test

The smudges are not in the picture. Every white line has the same width of 12 units and the squares are all 50 units across; the lines are pure white all the way along, including at the crossings.

Where it comes from

The German physiologist Ludimar Hermann described the grid in 1870 after noticing it in a physics book that he was reading. It is one of the oldest known illusions of brightness.

Why it happens

For many decades the usual explanation involved lateral inhibition: cells in the retina that respond to bright spots are held back by their neighbours, and a crossing has more dark neighbours than a plain line. More recent research suggests that explanation is incomplete, and that processing in the brain, not only the eye, contributes. The fact that the smudge vanishes under direct gaze, where the cells are smaller, is part of the evidence for the old explanation but not all of it.

Try this

Look slowly along a white line and watch the grey smudges appear at crossings just beside where you are looking and disappear where you look. Make the picture smaller (move back) or larger (bring it close) and see where the effect is strongest. Try rotating your device by 45 degrees.

Question

Are the grey spots really there?

No. Every part of the white lines is the same white. The grey is produced by your own visual system.
