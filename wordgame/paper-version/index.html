Tijori with pen and paper: the two-player game

Long before screens, people played guessing games in which one player hides a number or a word and the other closes in on it, guess by guess, helped only by a short score. These games are traditional and belong to everybody. Tijori is our own member of that family, and it works very well on paper between two people: on a train, in a waiting room, at the back of a notebook. One player is the safe-keeper, the other the safe-cracker.

What you need

A sheet of paper and a pen for each player. Nothing else.

Setting up

  1. The safe-keeper thinks of a five-letter word and writes it down secretly. It should be a word both players know: no names, and by custom no plurals made by adding S.
  2. The safe-cracker draws a simple score sheet: three columns headed Guess, Locked and Elsewhere, and eight rows.
  3. Below the sheet, the safe-cracker writes the alphabet in a line, to cross letters off.

Playing

  1. The safe-cracker says a five-letter word and writes it in the first row.
  2. The safe-keeper compares it with the secret word and answers in two parts:
    • Locked: which letters are correct and in the correct place. In our rules the keeper names them: "the A, third letter".
    • Elsewhere: how many of the other letters are in the secret word in a different place. Only the number is said.
  3. The cracker writes the answers down, crosses off letters that are proved absent, and guesses again.
  4. The cracker wins by saying the secret word within eight guesses. Then the players swap roles.

How the keeper scores a guess without mistakes

Scoring by hand is where paper games go wrong, so use this routine every time. Write the guess directly under the secret word.

  1. Go along the five columns. Wherever the two letters match, the letter is locked: tick it, and strike out that letter in both words.
  2. Now take each remaining letter of the guess in turn. If the same letter is still un-struck in the secret word, count one for "elsewhere" and strike out that letter in the secret word, so that it cannot be counted twice.
  3. Say the result.

Example: secret PLANT, guess TRAIN. Column three matches (A): locked. Remaining guess letters T, R, I, N; remaining secret letters P, L, N, T. T is there: one. R is not. I is not. N is there: two. The keeper says: "A is locked, third place; two elsewhere."

Honesty and checking

A wrong score ruins the game, because the cracker reasons from every answer. At the end, the keeper shows the secret word and the two players check each row together. If the keeper made a scoring mistake, the keeper loses the round. This rule makes keepers careful.

Scoring a match

Play an even number of rounds so that each player cracks as often as they keep. The cracker scores the number of guesses used; a failure counts 10. Lowest total wins. For a quick game, play two rounds; for an evening, six.

Variations

  • Harder: numbers only. The keeper gives two numbers, how many locked and how many elsewhere, without naming the locked letters. This is the classic form of the old pen-and-paper game, and it is much harder; allow twelve guesses.
  • Easier: four letters. For younger children use four-letter words and six guesses.
  • Both at once. Each player hides a word, and they take turns to guess at each other's. The first to crack the other's safe wins. No waiting, and the tension is doubled.
  • Hindi. Use three-akshar words, where an akshar with its matra counts as one unit, as in our Hindi rules.
  • Team game for a class. The teacher is the keeper and writes guesses and scores on the board; rows of pupils take turns to propose a guess.

Tips for the keeper: choosing a hard word

Words with a repeated letter, words from a large family that differ in one letter (see tricky words), and words with an unusual shape such as a vowel at the start are the hardest to crack. But choose a word your opponent knows; a word nobody has heard of is no fun to lose to.

Tips for the cracker

Everything in our strategy guide applies. On paper it helps to keep three lists under the score sheet: letters that are out, letters that are in, and for each letter that is in, the places where it cannot be.
