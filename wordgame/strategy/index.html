Tijori strategy: how to reason your way in

Tijori gives you less than some word puzzles do: it shows which letters are in the right place, but for the rest it only gives a count. That makes it a game of deduction. You are not hunting for the word so much as designing small experiments, each of which settles a question. Here is the method that works.

1. Open with a prepared word

Use a first guess with five different, fairly common letters, placed where they usually stand. Our calculation puts SHARE at the top; the starting words page lists twenty good ones. Do not agonise over the choice. The skill of the game lies in what you do next.

2. Treat a zero as a gift

A result of 0 feels like a miss. It is the most informative single result there is: five letters are gone for certain, and the keyboard greys them for you. After a zero, play five new letters.

3. Read the count by changing one thing

This is the heart of the game. Suppose STONE scores "+2 elsewhere" with no lock. Two of S, T, O, N, E are in the word; you do not know which. Now guess a word that keeps some of these letters and replaces others, and compare the totals. (Count locks and loose letters together as "letters found".)

  • If you swap one letter for a new one and the total falls by one, the letter you dropped is in the word and the new one is not.
  • If the total rises by one, the dropped letter is out and the new one is in.
  • If the total stays the same, the two letters are both in or both out. A later guess will tell you which.

Real words rarely differ by exactly one letter in a convenient way, so in practice you change two or three letters and reason about the possibilities. The principle is the same: the fewer things you change, the clearer the answer.

4. Move letters to find their places

A letter counted as "elsewhere" is in the word but not where you put it. Each time you play it in a new place without a lock, you rule out that place too. A letter can stand in only five places; after two or three misses its home is nearly fixed. Use the table of letter positions to try the likeliest place first.

5. Use the marks

Do not try to hold all this in your head. Tap Mark letters and grey out each letter as you rule it out; ring each one you prove. When a guess has "+1 elsewhere" and you have already greyed four of its letters, the fifth must be the one: ring it. Marks are what turn a pile of results into a short list of candidates.

6. Count the candidates before you guess

From the fourth guess on, ask before each move: how many words still fit everything I know? If the answer is one or two, guess. If it is five, do not guess one of them and hope. Play a test word that contains letters from several candidates, so that its result splits them. This is explained with an example in tricky words.

7. Remember what guesses are for

You have 8 tries. Opening the safe on the third is pleasant but worth nothing extra compared with failing on the eighth. So early guesses should be chosen to learn, not to be right. A typical good game looks like this: two or three guesses that sweep through fifteen or so letters; one or two that fix positions; then the answer.

8. Keep the hint for a real jam

The single hint opens the first shut dial. It is most valuable when you know which letters are in the word but cannot order them, or when you are caught in a family of look-alike words and the hint would open the dial that differs. Used on the first move it tells you little.

Common mistakes

  • Re-using a greyed letter. Every dead letter in a guess is a wasted slot, unless you are using it deliberately as a harmless filler in a test word.
  • Changing everything at once. If guess two shares no letters with guess one, you learn about ten letters separately, which is fine for a sweep. But if both score "+2", you still do not know which. At some point you must overlap.
  • Forgetting repeated letters. The count for a letter stops at the number of times it is in the word. A second E in your guess may score nothing even though E is in the word. And the answer itself may hold a double.
  • Putting a loose letter back in the same place. If it had been right there, it would have locked.
  • Guessing plurals. Words made by adding S are never the answer. They can be useful test words, but do not expect them to open the safe.

A short example of the one-letter change

First guess CRANE: "+1 elsewhere". Second guess CRAMP: 0. Every letter of CRAMP is out, so C, R and A are out, and the one letter found by CRANE was N or E. Third guess, to separate them and test new letters: TONIC. If N is the one, TONIC will show it. Three guesses in, you know the fate of eleven letters. That is the pace to aim for.

Put it to work on today's puzzle, or without pressure in practice mode.
