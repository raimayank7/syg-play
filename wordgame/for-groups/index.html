Playing Tijori as a group

A daily puzzle is more fun when someone else is doing it too. Everybody who opens Tijori on the same date gets the same word, which makes it a ready-made shared challenge for a family, a group of friends, an office or a class. Here are ways to set that up, with a few rules that keep it friendly.

A daily league in a WhatsApp group

  1. Agree on the game. Everyone plays the daily puzzle of the date on their own phone.
  2. Share only the result. After finishing, tap Share result and send the sentence to the group. It gives the puzzle number and your number of tries, and says if you used the hint. It never shows the word or your guesses.
  3. Keep the table. One person keeps the week's scores. Simple scoring: your number of tries is your score, a failed puzzle counts as 9, a hint adds 1. Lowest total on Sunday wins.
  4. Set a closing time. For example 9 pm. After that the word may be discussed.

Because the result is a plain sentence, it is easy to read aloud and does not flood the chat with pictures.

The one rule that matters: no spoilers

Agree that nobody posts the word, a guess or a "it rhymes with..." clue before the closing time. Screenshots of a finished game show the word, so those wait too. A group that respects this will still be playing in a year.

Together on one screen

For a family evening, open practice mode on a television, laptop or one phone passed round.

  • Co-operative: everyone discusses and one person types. The rule is that whoever proposes a guess must say what it would prove. This is the best way to teach the game to a newcomer or a child.
  • Turn by turn: players take the guesses in order. Whoever types the guess that opens the safe scores a point; then the next game starts with the next player.
  • Two teams, two devices: use the archive. Both teams choose the same past date and start at "go". The first to open the safe wins the round. Play best of five dates.

In the classroom

Tijori suits English lessons from about class 5 upward, and it has something for a maths teacher too.

  • Vocabulary and spelling. Every guess must be a real, correctly spelt five-letter word. Pupils search their memory for words that fit a pattern, which is active recall of exactly the right kind.
  • Reasoning aloud. Play one puzzle on the board. Before each guess, ask a pupil to state what is known ("no S, T or E; A is third; one of R and N is in the word") and what the proposed guess would test. The discipline of changing one thing at a time is the scientific method in miniature.
  • Pairs. Give each pair a practice puzzle on one device. One types, the other keeps notes on paper; they swap for the next word.
  • Without devices. The pen-and-paper version needs nothing but a notebook, and has the pupils do the scoring themselves, which teaches the rule thoroughly.

For elders and beginners

Start in practice mode, with no clock and no streak to worry about. Play the first few games co-operatively. Explain the zero early ("none of those letters, cross them all off"), since it is the easiest result to understand and the most encouraging to receive.

Hindi with the family

The Hindi Tijori uses everyday three-akshar words and can be a pleasant way for children who study in English to play with Hindi spelling and matras alongside parents and grandparents.

Fair play

  • Word finders and pattern solvers will open any safe in three tries. If your group competes, agree that they are not used during a game. Afterwards they are a fine way to learn what you missed.
  • The puzzle changes at midnight on each device. Members in other countries may be on a different date; compare puzzle numbers, which the shared sentence always shows.
  • Streaks and results live in each person's browser. They are for the player's own pleasure, not proof of anything.
