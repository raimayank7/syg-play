Alternating sequences: Two steps repeated

An alternating sequence uses two different steps in turn: for example add 5, subtract 2, add 5, subtract 2. The terms go up and down, but rise overall if the first step is bigger.

How to spot it

Look at the odd-numbered steps and the even-numbered steps separately. If the gaps go up by 5, down by 2, up by 5, down by 2, that is the pattern. Another kind uses two interleaved sequences, one at odd positions and one at even positions.

Step by step

Take the sequence 12, 18, 16, 22, 20. Step 1: write the gaps between neighbours: 6, -2, 6, -2. Step 2: write the gaps of the gaps: -8, 8, -8. Step 3: write the ratios of neighbours: 1.5, 0.89, 1.38, 0.91. Compare the three lines. The line that is steady (all the same) or follows a simple pattern tells you the rule. Here the rule is: add 6, then subtract 2, and repeat, so the next number is 26.

Common mistakes

Do not read the sequence as one pattern. Split it into odd positions and even positions, or look at the gaps in pairs. A sequence that goes up, down, up, down is rarely a single simple rule.

Where you meet this kind of sequence

Alternating steps appear in everyday patterns such as a person who walks three steps forward and one back, or a bank balance with a regular deposit and a regular fee. Puzzle setters use them to test whether you look at the pattern in pairs.
