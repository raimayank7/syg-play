Square number sequences: The squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25

A square number is a whole number multiplied by itself: 3 × 3 = 9. In a sequence of squares the gaps between terms are consecutive odd numbers: 3, 5, 7, 9 and so on. Sometimes the whole sequence is shifted, for example every square plus 2.

How to spot it

If the gaps are 3, 5, 7, 9 (odd numbers going up by 2), you have squares. Use the gaps to find the next one: add the next odd number. If the terms are each 1 or 2 away from a known square, a shifted sequence is likely.

Step by step

Take the sequence 16, 25, 36, 49, 64. Step 1: write the gaps between neighbours: 9, 11, 13, 15. Step 2: write the gaps of the gaps: 2, 2, 2. Step 3: write the ratios of neighbours: 1.56, 1.44, 1.36, 1.31. Compare the three lines. The line that is steady (all the same) or follows a simple pattern tells you the rule. Here the rule is: these are 4², 5², 6² and so on, so the next number is 81.

Common mistakes

The mistake is to take the gaps as the rule and add the same gap again. In squares the gaps change: 3, 5, 7. Also remember that a shifted square sequence (squares plus 2) has the same gaps as plain squares.

Where you meet this kind of sequence

Square numbers measure area: a square of side 5 has area 25. They appear in tile patterns, in chessboards (64 squares) and in the formula of Pythagoras for right-angled triangles.
